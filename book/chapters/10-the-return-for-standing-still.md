# Chapter 10: The Return for Standing Still

*Carry spans equities, currencies, bonds, commodities, and volatility futures, and the return it pays requires nothing to happen. Structural counterparties supply the premium because they value certainty over expected return. The premium decays when capital crowds the same structural trade, regenerates when policy cycles restore rate differentials, and crashes when margin calls on leverage liquidate positions before the accumulated carry compensates for the tail risk you accepted by holding them.*

Fifteen basis points. That was the Bank of Japan's rate hike in August 2024, and it triggered an estimated $250 billion unwind across global markets. Currency positions held for months on the promise of a steady interest differential collapsed in days. Yen carry traders who had been collecting their daily increment of interest, patiently and apparently safely, watched years of accumulated profit reverse in a week. That is what carry looks like on the wrong side of a policy decision smaller than the rounding error on most economic forecasts.

Think about what that premium looks like on a quiet night. You hold a position past the close. Nothing prints. No earnings surprise, no central bank statement, no geopolitical headline crosses the wire. Next morning the market opens within a fraction of a percent of where it settled. Your position has gone nowhere. And yet the account balance ticked up, automatically, as reliably as a meter running backward. That credit is carry. It showed up while price sat still.

Every other edge in this book depends on movement. Breakouts wait for a range to crack open. Trend trades need a direction that keeps going. Mean-reversion setups need a price stretched past its anchor. Carry asks for the opposite: nothing. And nothing is what most trading days deliver. The vast majority of instruments close within a percent or two of where they opened on any given session. Headlines belong to the dramatic days. The ordinary ones pay the carry.

Clenow states it so plainly that you might miss how much work the sentence does. Carry is the return you collect if price stays exactly where it is.<sup>[1]</sup> For equities, that is the dividend minus funding cost. For foreign exchange, it is the gap between the rate on the currency you own and the rate on the currency you borrowed. For futures, it is the distance from spot to the next contract, scaled by time remaining and normalized by the price of the underlying.

One definition. The arithmetic shifts from asset class to asset class. The logic does not.

Buy whatever pays you to hold it. Sell whatever charges you to own it. When the world sits still, the position profits. When the world moves, the carry either cushions a loss or stacks on top of a gain you already had. No forecast required. No directional view embedded. A structural return baked into the price of the instrument, sitting there for anyone willing to calculate it and hold through time.

Most traders walk straight past it. They chase breakouts, run momentum screens, scan for the next catalyst, while carry accumulates in the background, one quiet increment after the next, compounding without headlines. For the better part of fifty years, that silent accumulation delivered outsized returns.

## What You Earn for Doing Nothing

One formula works everywhere, and that is the remarkable part. Clenow applies it across equities, foreign exchange, futures, and their derivatives in a single framework.<sup>[1]</sup> Equities yield their dividend minus funding cost. FX yields the interest-rate differential between the currency you hold and the currency you borrow. Futures yield the spread between spot and the contract price, divided by time remaining, with backwardation producing positive carry and contango producing negative carry.

Strip the decision down to a single test. If annualized carry comes in positive, you hold long. If it comes in negative, you hold short. No direction to forecast, no momentum to interpret, no news to read. The position exists for the structural income it throws off while price goes nowhere. Clenow draws the line between this and trend-following explicitly: trend pays when price moves, carry pays when it does not. The two signals are conceptually orthogonal. Positive trend can sit alongside negative carry, or the reverse, and a systematic portfolio running both lets them reinforce where they agree and cancel where they clash.

You can access carry through almost any wrapper. Futures need a margin account and exchange membership. CFDs and spread bets, standard in the UK, replicate the same exposure under different regulatory and tax rules. Equities need nothing beyond a brokerage account and a schedule of dividend dates. Sophistication is not the barrier. Patience is. You run the calculation, you enter the position, and you wait.

**Reconstruction.** A dividend yield is published data. An interest-rate differential is broadcast by every central bank on earth. A futures term structure is visible on any trading screen. No proprietary model. No speed advantage. No privileged data feed. The carry premium sits on every financial terminal, available to anyone with a spreadsheet and a brokerage account, and yet it persists. Information advantage cannot explain that.

The answer is structural, and the structure is a counterparty. Every time a carry position collects its daily increment, someone on the other side pays that increment, not because they miscalculated, but because they have a reason to accept the cost. Corporate treasurers, commodity producers, central banks, pension funds, portfolio insurers. Each has a mandate that makes the cost acceptable and the payment repeatable. The mandates survive. The premium follows.

That distinction matters when you are the one holding the position through a drawdown. A premium whose source you understand survives, because you know why it will come back. A yield whose source you cannot explain leaves you defenseless the moment the account marks down. What would disprove the structural story is a carry premium that persists in an asset class with no constrained counterparty. No such case appears in the record.

**What it takes.** You need a brokerage account, a margin facility if trading futures, and a dividend calendar or term-structure screen for the asset class you choose. No proprietary data, no speed advantage, no model. The carry is published. The barrier is not access but tolerance: the position sits open for months or years collecting small increments while the account occasionally marks down by multiples of what has been earned. A single adverse move can erase a year of accumulated carry in a week, and no stop-loss is built into the framework. The disqualifier is sizing to the yield rather than to the drawdown.

## The Other Side of the Trade

Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen, and Vrugt published a study titled simply "Carry" in the Journal of Financial Economics in 2017, and the scope of it was the point.<sup>[2]</sup> They decomposed expected return into carry plus expected price appreciation and showed that carry predicts future returns cross-sectionally and in time series for global equities, global bonds, commodities, US Treasuries, credit, and options. A single global carry factor spans all of them. The finding that matters: carry is not a proxy for other known predictors. It adds explanatory power even after controlling for value and momentum.

You can run the same signal across six asset classes, and it tells you something about expected returns that no other standard factor captures. The question is who keeps paying.

Start with corporate hedgers. A multinational earning foreign currency revenue buys forward contracts each quarter to nail down exchange rates, and the forward points it pays are precisely the interest-rate differential between the two currencies. The motive is fiduciary, regulatory, or written into an internal risk committee charter. Picture the corporate treasurer at an automobile manufacturer shipping cars to Japan. That treasurer does not pause each quarter to evaluate whether uncovered interest parity holds. The forward gets bought because the board insists on earnings certainty, because the audit committee demands documented hedging, because the CFO's bonus rides on stable margins. Return seeking plays no part. The cost of that certainty, paid quarterly without pushback, is the carry you collect.<sup>[2]</sup>

Next come commodity producers. Oil companies and agricultural exporters sell futures to guarantee revenue, and the theory explaining why dates back almost a century. Normal backwardation, first articulated by Keynes in 1930 and formalized by Hicks in 1939, holds that this hedging pressure is the primary reason commodity futures earn a risk premium at all. A wheat farmer locking in December futures at a discount to the expected harvest-time spot price is not speculating on grain. That farmer is buying certainty for the business plan. You, as the futures buyer, provide insurance against price decline. Your expected profit is the premium for shouldering that insurance.<sup>[2]</sup>

Then central banks. For decades the Bank of Japan held its policy rate near zero, flooding the world with cheap yen funding as a byproduct of chasing a domestic inflation target. The BoJ was not engineering a carry subsidy. It was trying to reflate the Japanese economy. But what it created was a standing invitation: borrow yen, buy anything with a higher yield. Hundreds of billions of dollars worth of carry trades ran on that single funding leg. When the BoJ eventually moved, the unwind was spectacular. The subsidy, though, had been running for decades before anyone called it.

Sovereign wealth funds and reserve managers occupy a fourth tier. A central bank stockpiling dollar reserves is not placing a carry bet; it is building a buffer against balance-of-payments crises. A sovereign wealth fund parking capital in US Treasuries at modest yields is not chasing alpha; it is storing national wealth in the deepest, most liquid market available. Inferior expected returns are not a miscalculation for these institutions. Accepting those returns is the point of their mandate. They absorb currency and duration exposure that carry traders shed, stabilizing the premium in ordinary times and concentrating the risk into the moments when they pull back.

A fifth explanation comes from Farhi and Gabaix, who argued in a 2009 NBER working paper that high-yield currencies carry heavier exposure to a global disaster risk factor than low-yield currencies. The premium, in their framework, is rational compensation for bearing that tail exposure. The counterparty is not a single institution but the market's collective pricing of rare, catastrophic global downturns. Negative skewness in carry returns is not a flaw in the payoff. It is the payoff.

**Reconstruction.** Five counterparties, and not one of them is making an error. The corporate treasurer executes a mandate. The commodity producer locks revenue. The central bank targets inflation. The sovereign wealth fund stores national savings. The disaster-risk framework prices the tail. Each pays the carry premium for a reason that has nothing to do with expected return and everything to do with a constraint that will not loosen when the premium widens.

The premium is not a market inefficiency waiting to be arbitraged away. It is an equilibrium in which constrained institutions systematically transfer wealth to unconstrained investors in exchange for risk they cannot afford to retain. The transfer is voluntary, repeated, and mandated. You are not exploiting a mistake. You are providing a service, and the service is absorbing the risk that someone else's mandate forbids them from holding.

Extend the question to volatility markets and the same structure reappears. Corporations buy options to hedge quarterly earnings risk, pension funds buy tail protection to satisfy regulatory requirements, and insurance companies hedge equity-linked liabilities. The carry counterparty is always someone forced to pay for certainty. Forward contract, futures position, option premium: the instrument changes, the mechanism does not. What would break this pattern is a shift in mandate structure large enough to remove the constrained counterparty entirely from the market. No such shift has occurred in any of the six asset classes the academic literature documents.

**What it takes.** The carry premium spans six asset classes, and accessing more than one requires multi-market brokerage, margin agreements across equity, fixed-income, and derivatives, and enough capital that structural positions are worth maintaining after hedging costs. A single-market carry position concentrates the crash risk into one counterparty relationship. The constraint that ends most carry portfolios is survival through the unwind: the counterparties are mandated to keep paying, and you are not mandated to keep holding.

## Harvesting the VIX Curve

The VIX futures curve spends roughly 80% of its time in contango. Chan builds a basis trade on that single structural fact.<sup>[3]</sup> No directional view on the equity market enters the calculation. The trigger is precise: calculate the daily roll as the front VIX futures price minus the VIX spot index, divided by the number of days until settlement. Above 0.10 VIX points per day, the trade fires.

What contango means in practice is that the front futures contract sits above fair value relative to where the VIX spot index will likely settle at expiration. Your move is to short that front contract and offset the directional equity exposure by going long ES mini S&P 500 futures. The hedge ratio falls out of a regression of VIX futures price changes on S&P 500 percentage changes, which strips out the inverse relationship between volatility and equities. Five business days later you close both legs, regardless of where VIX or the S&P 500 landed in the interim.<sup>[3]</sup>

In backwardation, the logic reverses. The front contract trades below spot, indicating elevated current fear, and the trade goes long VIX futures while shorting ES minis as the hedge.

The holding period is fixed. The entry threshold is fixed. The hedge ratio is determined by regression. No discretion anywhere in the execution. The carry is embedded in the term structure, not in your judgment. The trade is as mechanical as a dividend payment, except that the dividend comes from term-structure convergence rather than a company's earnings.

Chan cites Simon and Campasano (2014) as the academic foundation for this convergence pattern, documenting the statistical reliability of VIX futures basis mean-reversion and the profitability of a systematic strategy exploiting it.<sup>[3]</sup> The academic paper provides what the practitioner framework requires. The basis is not random noise. It is a structural feature of how VIX futures are priced, driven by the persistent demand for volatility protection from equity portfolio managers who buy VIX-linked products to hedge their equity holdings.

**Reconstruction.** The 0.10 threshold is a filter, and the filter is doing more work than it appears to do. It screens out periods when contango is too thin to cover execution risk, overnight margin requirements on short VIX futures, and the residual basis risk that the regression hedge leaves uncaptured. Without it, you are selling a premium that may not exist after costs.

The premium it passes through is structural. Equity investors, pension funds, and portfolio insurers buy VIX-linked products as insurance, systematically paying above fair value because the alternative is worse. An unhedged crash costs more than the hedge. You collect the difference between what these buyers pay and what the VIX actually delivers. That is carry in its most transparent form. Counterparty identifiable. Mandate documented. Overpayment measurable on a daily basis.

The parallel to every other carry trade in this chapter is exact. FX carry: corporate treasurer buying forwards to hedge currency exposure. Commodity carry: producer selling futures to lock revenue. VIX carry: portfolio manager buying volatility insurance to protect equity holdings. Each pays a predictable cost to remove a specific risk from the balance sheet. What would show the VIX basis premium is not structural is a sustained period where contango disappears without a corresponding spike in realized volatility. The 80% contango frequency suggests that period has not arrived.

**What it takes.** If you cannot hold short VIX futures overnight with the margin your broker requires, this trade does not start. A futures account with exchange access to VIX and ES mini contracts, a regression model to compute the hedge ratio, and the infrastructure to monitor the daily roll and execute both legs simultaneously. The holding period is five business days, and the position cannot be abandoned mid-hold without breaking the convergence thesis. The risk that disqualifies most accounts is a VIX spike during the hold: contango flips to backwardation, the short VIX leg moves against you violently, and the ES hedge covers only the portion of the move the regression captured.

## Borrowing Cheap, Holding Dear

Borrow yen at 0.1%, convert to Australian dollars, earn 4%.<sup>[4]</sup> The differential is 3.9% annually, and it arrives for holding the position. Nothing else. If the Australian dollar appreciates against the yen, you collect the carry and the capital gain. If the Australian dollar sits flat, you still collect 3.9%. The interest flows into your account daily, as automatic as a utility meter running in reverse. A textbook on systematic trading describes this as the canonical FX carry trade in its simplest form, and the simplicity is the point.

Now flip it over. A sharp depreciation in the high-yield currency wipes out months or years of accumulated carry in days.<sup>[4]</sup> The textbook states this risk without attaching a probability or a magnitude. What holds the whole trade together is a single empirical observation: uncovered interest parity, the theory predicting that high-yield currencies should fall enough to neutralize the interest advantage, breaks down persistently over multi-year stretches. You collect the differential. The exchange rate, on average, does not take it back.

No timing signal is offered. No stop-loss is specified. No position-sizing rule constrains the exposure. The trade sits open, collecting its daily interest credit, until either the differential narrows or the market moves against you violently enough that the losses exceed the accumulated carry.

Consider what happens when you run this as a single pair. One exchange rate, one central bank, one policy cycle, and every gram of crash risk piled into that single position. Clenow's formalization treats carry differently: as one signal among many, applying the same decomposition (deposit rate minus funding rate) across all G10 pairs simultaneously.<sup>[1]</sup> The systematic version goes long the top quartile of carry pairs and short the bottom quartile, spreading exposure across multiple exchange rates and multiple central bank regimes. On top of that diversification, Clenow blends the carry signal with trend and momentum signals for position sizing, letting the system trim carry positions when trend turns negative, a rough but functional guard against the crash scenario.

That is the gap between a single differential and a cross-sectional harvest. Interest parity fails in both versions, so the structural edge is identical. The risk is not. A single-pair position can shed 35% in six months; AUD/JPY proved that in 2008. A diversified cross-sectional portfolio, with trend signals pulling exposure down when momentum flips negative, caps any one pair's contribution to the drawdown. What you choose determines how much crash risk concentrates in one exchange rate and whether anything in the system can reduce your exposure before the unwind arrives. That choice is not a footnote. It separates a strategy that survives its own tail from one that does not.

## When Commodities Paid Like Equities

Forty-five years of equity-like returns from commodity futures, and the mechanism was the term structure itself.<sup>[5]</sup> Gorton and Rouwenhorst published the evidence in the Financial Analysts Journal in 2006 under a title that announced the argument: "Facts and Fantasies About Commodity Futures." The fantasy was that commodities were too risky, too volatile, and too speculative for institutional portfolios. The fact was that fully collateralized commodity futures had earned returns comparable to equities over the period 1959 to 2004, with negative correlation to stocks and bonds. You could have held commodity futures alongside equities and earned similar returns with lower volatility and better drawdown characteristics than equities alone.

The carry came from backwardation. Tight inventories, a drought thinning the wheat supply, a refinery shutdown squeezing gasoline, push spot prices above futures prices. A long futures position earns roll yield because each expiring contract converges upward toward that higher spot price. When you roll into the next contract, you are buying at a discount and selling the old one at a premium. That spread is your carry, collected mechanically at every roll date.

Keynes named this normal backwardation in 1930, and the counterparty is the same constrained seller you keep meeting in every carry trade: commodity producers locking in future revenue by selling forward.<sup>[5]</sup> The buyer of those futures, first a commodity merchant and later an institutional investor, takes on the price risk. The expected profit from bearing that risk is the insurance premium. For forty-five years, that premium ran large enough to rival equity returns.

Then the market changed.

Passive index capital discovered commodity futures after 2004, and what it brought was volume without discrimination. Hundreds of billions poured into index funds that rolled long positions on a fixed schedule regardless of whether the curve was in backwardation or contango, regardless of inventory conditions. The mandate said buy futures, so they bought. The mandate said roll before expiration, so they rolled. That mechanical buying pushed market after market from backwardation into persistent contango, where futures sit above spot and long positions hemorrhage roll yield instead of collecting it.

Bhardwaj, Gorton, and Rouwenhorst documented the damage in a 2015 NBER working paper: returns were materially lower in the 2004 to 2014 decade, and they attributed the decline to the financialization of commodities.<sup>[6]</sup> The passive money was not earning the roll yield. It was destroying the roll yield for everyone, including itself.

**Reconstruction.** Every other edge decay in this book follows the same story: the signal leaks, competitors arrive, the alpha compresses. Commodity carry broke a different way. The hedging pressure that created backwardation did not disappear after 2004. Wheat farmers still sell futures and oil producers still lock in revenue. The supply side held. What collapsed was the other side of the trade.

Passive index money arrived in volume, hundreds of billions of dollars buying long-only commodity futures without conditioning on term structure, without checking whether backwardation still existed. The new capital bid away the backwardation premium by sheer weight, converting favorable roll yield into persistent contango bleed. No information leaked. No model was reverse-engineered. The capital itself transformed the pricing structure it was harvesting.

That distinction matters for you if you are choosing where to deploy carry capital. Cross-sectional commodity carry, long the backwardated markets and short the contangoed ones, still avoids the markets where passive money has poisoned the term structure. The long-only basket that collected roll yield for forty-five years is compressed, possibly permanently. What would disprove the crowding thesis is a reversal of passive inflows that restores widespread backwardation. No such reversal is visible in the data.

**What it takes.** Not a single commodity position but a cross-sectional portfolio: long the backwardated markets, short the contangoed ones, with infrastructure to track term structures across a dozen or more commodity futures simultaneously. Margin accounts at an exchange that clears commodity futures, and enough capital to hold both legs through seasonal and inventory-driven swings. Running the simple long-only version and expecting the pre-2004 premium is the disqualifier: hundreds of billions in passive index money converted the term structure, and the contango bleed is now a standing cost for anyone who holds the basket without checking whether backwardation still exists.

## Yield Curve Convergence

Fixed-income carry trades on the gap between where yield-curve spreads sit and where they tend to settle.<sup>[7]</sup> A systematic trading text describes the logic: identify distortions in the yield curve, short the overpriced maturity, buy the underpriced one. When credit spreads widen beyond historical norms, you buy corporate bonds and short government paper, waiting for the spread to mean-revert. When swap rates diverge from bond yields, you arbitrage the gap.

No specific performance figures are offered. No named time period is cited. No Sharpe ratio, no drawdown, no capacity estimate. The evidence quality is asserted rather than documented.<sup>[7]</sup>

The convergence version requires judgment about what constitutes a distortion. Carver's formalization requires none. His framework treats yield-curve carry as one component of a multi-signal forecast, where the carry from the slope of the curve, the difference between long-term and short-term yields, is combined with trend and value signals to produce a position-sizing recommendation. The signal is the yield differential itself, measured mechanically and applied across dozens of instruments.

**Reconstruction.** The trade is real, and the participants are identifiable: fixed-income relative-value desks at banks and hedge funds run variants of this convergence bet continuously. The carry component is the yield differential between the two legs. The convergence component is the expected mean reversion of the spread. Both are measurable, and neither is the problem.

The problem is leverage. Spread differences between maturities or credit grades are measured in basis points, not percentage points. To generate a meaningful absolute return from a 20-basis-point compression, you need 10x or 50x leverage. The leverage itself creates the tail risk that erases years of accumulated carry in a single liquidity crisis. That is the mechanism you are signing up for.

The precedent is documented. A fund collecting bond carry with extraordinary Sharpe ratios ran for years before a sovereign default destroyed the strategy within weeks. The daily returns looked like a money market fund. The tail event looked like bankruptcy. The carry was real, and the leverage was fatal. What would falsify this as a general pattern is a bond-carry portfolio that survives a liquidity crisis at the leverage ratios the returns require. No such example appears in the public record.

**What it takes.** Ten to fifty times leverage, because the spread differences between maturities or credit grades are measured in basis points, and meaningful returns at that scale require a prime broker willing to extend it. Simultaneous access to government and corporate bond markets, or to swaps and bonds, with margin arrangements at both. No performance data is offered for this trade in the source, no Sharpe ratio, no drawdown path. The margin calls that leverage generates arrive precisely when the spread widens further instead of converging, and no documented ceiling exists for how far the spread can go before it narrows.

## A Practitioner's Dividend Machine

Yield between 4% and 6%, dividends climbing year over year, a price-to-earnings ratio sitting between 8 and 15, and a business that sells something people pay for regardless of what the economy does.<sup>[8]</sup> Utilities. Telecoms. Essential services. Burns runs a dividend portfolio on those filters and nothing else, applied over decades. Buy near the bottom of the five-year trading range. Size each holding at £5,000 to £10,000. Hold for five years or longer. Collect the income. Add on dips inside the established range. Do not trade. A 20% to 25% stop-loss guards against genuine fundamental deterioration, not against the market having a bad quarter.

Telecom Plus, a UK utility company, has been in the portfolio since 2001, and the cumulative dividend income from that single position exceeds £400,000.<sup>[8]</sup> The portfolio as a whole generates £120,000 in annual dividend income. Those are practitioner-reported figures from a single source, so hold them at that weight.

Capital gains show up and they are welcome. They are not the point. Burns frames it plainly: a 5% annual dividend yield, reinvested or collected, doubles your capital in roughly fourteen years even if the share price never moves.<sup>[8]</sup> The share price tracks sentiment. The dividend is the return.

The stillness has a condition. The company must remain fundamentally sound. A dividend cut converts a 5% yield into a trap. A balance-sheet deterioration converts a defensive stock into a value sink. A regulatory shift can strand a whole sector overnight, eliminating both the income and the capital base. Burns sets the threshold at 20% to 25% below entry. That stop does not protect against price fluctuation. It protects against the scenario where the business model generating those dividends has broken permanently. When it fires, close the position, take the loss, redeploy the capital into the next boring, defensive, high-yielding name on the screen.

**Reconstruction.** The counterparty here is not a forced hedger, a mandated institution, or a central bank running a rate policy. That makes equity dividend carry structurally different from every other carry trade in this chapter. The premium persists because equity markets chronically overprice excitement. Growth investors pay for potential capital appreciation, compressing dividend yields on high-growth stocks and leaving boring, defensive, low-growth equities with a yield that patient capital can collect. The edge is behavioral, not informational. Screeners are free. Dividend yields are published.

Twenty-two years holding one utility stock is not a trade. It is a structural position that compounds quietly while the rest of the market generates turnover, pays commissions, and absorbs impact costs. You do nothing. The nothing is the return.

But the nothing has a prerequisite you cannot verify in advance: continued fundamental soundness across an entire business cycle. A sector-wide regulatory change, a balance-sheet event, a dividend suspension, any of these ends the compounding and starts the loss. The stop-loss is the only mechanism that addresses this, and it fires after the damage, not before it. What would disprove the behavioral-counterparty thesis is a defensive equity market where boring stocks trade at the same valuation multiples as growth stocks. No such market has appeared in the record.

**What it takes.** A brokerage account, a stock screener filtering for 4% to 6% yield, rising dividends, and a PE between 8 and 15. Capital to build positions at £5,000 to £10,000 per holding near the bottom of a five-year range. No leverage, no derivatives, no proprietary data. The time horizon is five years at a minimum, and the documented example runs twenty-two. A 20% to 25% stop-loss is the only exit rule, and it fires on fundamental deterioration, not price fluctuation. What disqualifies is the horizon: holding a position for a decade while the rest of the market generates turnover and headlines, and taking the loss when the stop says the dividend stream is broken.

## When Carry Breaks

Small, frequent gains and rare, violent losses: that is the return profile Brunnermeier, Nagel, and Pedersen documented in their 2008 NBER working paper, and it defined the academic understanding of carry crash risk.<sup>[9]</sup> Carry returns exhibit persistent negative skewness. The gains arrive in increments that look like monthly interest payments. The losses arrive in episodes that erase months or years of accumulated profit in days. The distribution is not symmetric, and the asymmetry is not incidental. It is the product.

The trigger is not a change in fundamentals. It is a margin call. When a risk-off shock hits, a credit crisis, a policy surprise, a geopolitical event, and you are holding a leveraged carry position, the broker does not wait for your analysis. Traders sell their high-yield holdings and buy back their funding currencies, which drives the high-yield side down further, which triggers more margin calls, which forces more selling. A self-reinforcing cascade. The yen still yields 0.1%. The Australian dollar still yields 4%. The interest-rate differential has not moved. But the exchange rate has moved violently enough that positions are liquidated at losses large enough to wipe out years of accumulated interest.

The 2008 episode is the canonical case: the Australian dollar fell approximately 35% against the Japanese yen in the six months following the Lehman Brothers collapse.<sup>[9]</sup> A decade of accumulated carry reversed in weeks. Not because the interest-rate outlook changed. Because the margin-call cascade did not wait for an outlook.

The 2024 episode updated the template. A single Bank of Japan policy signal, a 15-basis-point rate hike, triggered an estimated unwind of 40 trillion yen, roughly $250 billion, within days.<sup>[10]</sup> The BIS documented the event in Bulletin No. 90: the largest single-day VIX spike since 2020, forced liquidation rippling across equity, credit, and commodity markets, contagion reaching asset classes with no direct connection to the yen carry trade. Markets recovered within weeks. Yen carry positions were rebuilt by late 2024.<sup>[10]</sup>

Fifteen basis points. A rate hike smaller than the rounding error on most economic forecasts. That was the size of the policy change that set off a quarter-trillion-dollar unwind across global markets. If you held yen carry through that week, you already know the number felt larger than it reads.

Watch what happened next. Within weeks, markets stabilized. Within months, yen carry positions were being rebuilt. By late 2024, the trade was back, the differential restored, the structural incentive intact. The crash did not end carry. It reset it. The same sequence played out after 2008: the Australian dollar recovered, the interest differential persisted, and carry traders re-entered on the other side of the drawdown, chastened but not deterred. You can read that as resilience or as amnesia. The market does not distinguish.

The premium regenerates. The crash regenerates too.

Before 2008, academic estimates put annualized Sharpe ratios for simple G10 carry strategies at 0.6 to 0.7. After 2008, the dollar-funded Sharpe ratio fell to near zero. Three forces drove the compression: near-zero policy rates across the developed world from 2009 to 2021 crushed the differentials that generate FX carry; crowding among institutional alternative-risk-premia funds made the trade more fragile, since everyone held the same positions and exited through the same door; and post-2008 options markets began pricing carry-crash risk more richly, raising the cost of hedging and shrinking the net risk-adjusted return of any protected carry portfolio. If you ran a simple carry basket through that decade, the return you collected barely compensated for the tail you accepted.

The 2022 to 2024 hiking cycle changed the picture again, restoring large differentials between the US and Japan, between high-yield emerging markets and the developed world. The trade revived to an estimated $250 billion in yen-funded positions alone before unwinding again in August 2024. The revival was swift, and the rebuilding after the unwind was swifter. That speed tells you something about how persistent the structural incentive remains when your funding rate sits near zero and your target rate sits at four or five percent.

**Reconstruction.** Carry is not dead, and neither is its crash. The premium has meaningfully weakened in G10 FX since 2008, and the weakening tracks three identifiable forces: collapsed rate differentials, crowding among risk-premia funds, and richer options pricing on carry-crash protection. Each of those forces is regime-dependent. When differentials return, the premium returns. When crowding thins, the unwind softens. None of them is permanent.

That is the durability verdict, and it is more nuanced than a binary. Carry is regime-dependent. It appears when policy cycles create differentials and compresses when those differentials collapse. The premium is genuine compensation for bearing disaster risk, and the disaster arrives on a schedule set by central bank policy, not by any calendar you can read in advance.

The question you face is not whether the premium is real. Six decades of academic evidence across multiple asset classes settle that. The question is whether the margin call arrives before the accumulated carry compensates you for bearing the tail risk. In 2008, the 35% AUD/JPY decline erased years of 3-4% annual carry in six months. It did. In August 2024, it nearly did: only the speed of the recovery saved the positions that survived the initial margin calls. Carry rewards patience and punishes leverage, and the interaction between those two forces determines whether the premium is harvestable in practice, not merely demonstrable in theory.

**What it takes.** You are holding a position whose small daily gain can reverse into a loss that erases years of accumulation in a week. An FX account with margin sufficient to survive the initial spike without forced liquidation, because the margin call arrives before you can evaluate whether the differential has actually changed. The 2008 AUD/JPY decline was 35% in six months against 3-4% annual carry. The 2024 yen unwind was $250 billion in days against a 15-basis-point trigger. Diversification across multiple carry pairs and combination with trend signals reduce the concentration, but the tail risk is structural and cannot be hedged away without consuming the premium. No carry portfolio survives a crash it was sized to survive only on paper.

## Key Points

- Carry is the return collected when price does not move; it persists because structural counterparties value certainty over expected return.
- Corporate hedgers, commodity producers, and central banks supply the carry premium through mandated, non-return-seeking activity.
- The VIX basis trade harvests term-structure decay through convergence, demonstrating carry in its most transparent and hedgeable form.
- Commodity carry earned equity-like returns for decades before passive index inflows converted backwardation to contango and compressed the roll yield.
- Equity dividend carry compounds through time but requires a horizon measured in decades and a fundamentally sound business.
- Carry crashes are triggered by margin calls on leverage, not by changes in the underlying fundamentals that create the premium.
- The premium is regime-dependent: it revives when policy cycles restore rate differentials and compresses when those differentials collapse.

A 15-basis-point rate hike triggered a $250 billion unwind. Carry pays for bearing disaster risk, but the margin call arrives before the insurance payout. The return for standing still is real, documented across six decades and six asset classes. The question is not whether the premium exists. It is whether you can survive the crash that comes between the carry payments. If carry is the return for standing still, the next question is what edge pays for selling insurance explicitly.
