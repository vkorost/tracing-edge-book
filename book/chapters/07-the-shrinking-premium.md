# Chapter 7: The Shrinking Premium

*Ten percent annual returns from sorting stocks by last year's winners and losers. That was the cross-sectional momentum premium in the early 1990s, and it drew capital the way a published treasure map draws prospectors. By 2025 the premium sits near two percent, compressed by the same machines and mandates that arrived to harvest it. The signal is not dead. It is public, crowded, and almost entirely consumed by the cost of capturing it. What follows is a case study in what happens to any edge once enough capital shows up to agree that it exists.*

## The Ranking Machine

Sort every stock by its trailing twelve-month return, buy the top decile, short the bottom decile, hold for a year, rebalance, and repeat. In the early 1990s that trade produced annual returns in the neighborhood of ten percent, a number so large and so persistent that it survived every robustness check the academic literature could throw at it and still looked like free money to anyone managing a portfolio. Product managers noticed. Quantitative allocators noticed. Index providers noticed and built momentum benchmarks so that passive vehicles could track the signal on autopilot, rebalancing on a schedule, buying the winners and shorting the losers without a single person at the desk asking whether the premium could absorb the next billion dollars of inflow.

Open a 2025 textbook and the same strategy is still there, described in the same language. One number has changed. The documented premium now sits at roughly two percent.<sup>[1]</sup>

Four-fifths of the original return, consumed. Not by a replication failure, not by a methodological correction. By the weight of the capital that arrived to collect it. The arithmetic is straightforward: enough participants chasing the same top-decile names push those prices higher before the signal fires, and enough participants shorting the same bottom-decile names push those prices lower on the same schedule. By the time your ranking says buy, the crowd has already repositioned, and whatever the late arrivals scrape off the table is the residual sitting inside that two-percent figure.

Look at how that trajectory compares to the edges in the previous two chapters. Breakout patterns survive publication because execution skill and intraday timing introduce enough variation to preserve a practitioner-level advantage even after the setup appears in every textbook. Trend-following survives because decade-long flat stretches shake out impatient capital and periodically restore the premium for whoever stayed. Cross-sectional momentum follows neither pattern. It has compressed steadily and has not snapped back. The algorithms that harvest it do not lose patience and do not leave. They sit on the signal, cycle after cycle, grinding the premium a little thinner each year, and no drawdown arrives to clear them out because the strategy is embedded in index products that rebalance whether the annual return is ten percent or two percent or zero.

Nobody disputes that the premium exists. The question worth asking is whether anything useful remains once the capital that arrived to exploit it has finished compressing it, and whether you can still pull value from the signal through a different implementation of the same underlying idea.

**Reconstruction.** Ten percent to roughly two percent is the trajectory, and no single published study traces that exact compression in one clean time series carrying those round numbers. The framing reflects a broad consensus across multiple academic papers and practitioner commentary, directionally aligned with the McLean and Pontiff finding that published anomalies lose a significant share of their pre-publication returns once arbitrage capital arrives. The compression mechanism, capital crowding into the same signal until the excess return narrows, is not contested in the literature. What is contested is whether the residual compensates for the risk that momentum still carries. The 2009 crash, when long-short momentum spreads reversed violently as beaten-down stocks surged and prior winners collapsed, remains the standing exhibit. A residual of two percent per year that occasionally delivers a catastrophic quarterly drawdown may not be a premium at all. It may be compensation for bearing exactly that tail risk, which means the headline story, a premium that shrank, is incomplete: the return shrank but the risk did not, and the ratio between them is worse now than it was when the original research appeared. These figures are approximate, assembled from multiple sources rather than drawn from a single controlled measurement, and treating them as precise overstates what the evidence actually supports.

**What it takes.** Roughly two percent annual return, gross, before transaction costs and borrow fees on the short book. That is the documented residual from a premium that once ran near ten percent, and the first question is whether two percent covers the drag. A top-decile/bottom-decile long-short portfolio across a broad equity universe, rebalanced on a trailing twelve-month signal, with the infrastructure to short the bottom decile at scale. The short book is where most capital stalls: borrow availability, recall risk, and crowded positioning in the same bottom-decile names that every other momentum fund is also selling. Tail risk from momentum crashes, like the 2009 reversal, sits outside any backtest that stops before the crash year.

## The Factor Regression

Fama-French factors go in, predicted next-day returns come out, and a portfolio of one hundred names rebalances before the next session opens. That is one version of cross-sectional momentum formalized with academic machinery: regress each stock's historical returns against market risk, size, value, and their extensions, then let the fitted model pick the top fifty for the long book and the bottom fifty for the short book, dollar-neutral, refreshed daily.<sup>[1]</sup>

Nobody reads a chart. Nobody checks the tape. The regression produces predictions, the optimizer produces a portfolio, and the whole apparatus ticks forward without a single subjective call about whether a given name belongs on the sheet. That is the appeal of building momentum on published factors: you remove every discretionary input and let the academic model do the sorting. It is also the vulnerability, because every other quantitative shop with a terminal and an internet connection is running the same published coefficients against the same daily data, building a portfolio that holds the same fifty longs, shorts the same fifty names, and rebalances on the same overnight schedule.

The factors are not behind a paywall. You can pull them from a public website before your morning coffee cools. Every quantitative finance program on the planet teaches them, every backtest library ships them as sample data, and every factor portfolio constructed from them trades into the same crowded set of names on the same calendar. That convergence is not an accident and not a side effect. It is the direct consequence of building a strategy on inputs that are freely available, universally studied, and updated on a schedule everyone can read. The excess return that once rewarded the first movers has narrowed alongside the premium it was supposed to capture, squeezed by the transaction costs of daily rebalancing across a hundred positions and by the market impact of a hundred funds hitting the same order book at the same time.

**Reconstruction.** A named author with a long publication record in quantitative finance lays out this specific implementation on the QuantConnect platform. Backtested, not traded live, with no reported returns from an actual account. The architecture, daily factor regression across a hundred positions held dollar-neutral, is a textbook exercise in quantitative equity research and adds no methodological novelty. It appears here as an illustration of what happens when academic explanatory power meets a live order book: the Fama-French factors still explain cross-sectional return variation in a statistical sense, but explaining variation and capturing excess returns after transaction costs are distinct claims, and the gap between them has widened with every year of additional capital inflow. Two headwinds compound against anyone trying to profit from the explanatory power: the volume of capital deployed on the same signal, and the friction of rebalancing fifty longs and fifty shorts every session. Whatever edge persists has migrated entirely from the informational layer, where the factors were once a genuine discovery, to the operational layer, where the differentiator is execution cost, market impact per rebalance, and the ability to manage a short book's borrow expenses at scale. Those advantages accrue to the largest and cheapest participants, not to a newcomer reconstructing the model from the original papers.

**What it takes.** If you cannot rebalance one hundred positions daily without the transaction costs consuming the signal, the factor regression does not run. Fifty longs, fifty shorts, refreshed every session, dollar-neutral, built on Fama-French factors that are published, freely downloadable, and updated daily. The signal carries zero informational edge. Whatever remains accrues to the participant with the lowest execution cost and the smallest market impact per rebalance.

## The Slope and the Fit

Ninety days of prices, one exponential curve, and a single number that tells you whether the climb was real or an artifact. Andreas Clenow, writing in *Stocks on the Move*, builds his momentum ranking on that arithmetic: fit an exponential regression to each stock's ninety-day price series, measure the annualized slope, then multiply it by R-squared.<sup>[2]</sup>

Slope alone is not enough. A stock that gapped up thirty percent on an earnings surprise and spent the next sixty days going nowhere still shows a steep regression line. The steepness came from one morning, not from three months of buying pressure, and treating it as momentum is treating noise as signal. R-squared is the filter. It measures how much of the price action the fitted curve actually explains. High R-squared means the stock traced a smooth, steady path upward. Low R-squared means scatter, erratic jumps, a slope generated by a single discontinuity rather than by sustained demand. Multiply slope by R-squared and the gap-driven outlier drops through the ranking while the stock that gained the same amount in small, consistent steps rises to the top.

Steady climbers beat erratic leapers. That is the entire sorting logic.

Two additional gates stand between the ranking and the portfolio, and both are worth watching closely. Any stock trading below its hundred-day moving average is excluded. You can run a strong ninety-day regression and still be in a deteriorating longer-term trend, and the filter catches exactly that divergence. Any stock whose price moved more than fifteen percent in the lookback window because of a single event is also excluded. This is the gap filter applied a second time, now as a hard disqualifier rather than a statistical penalty.<sup>[2]</sup>

The universe is the S&P 500, and the output is a ranked list refreshed periodically. It answers one question: which names in the index are climbing most smoothly right now? Not most violently. Not most dramatically. Most smoothly. The distinction matters because smooth momentum is the variety most likely to persist. Explosive moves revert. Steady appreciation compounds.

**Reconstruction.** Institutional experience, published methodology, a replicable formula. Those three properties together sound like a strong evidence base, and in any other context they would be. Here they constitute the precise mechanism of the method's own decay. Clenow spent more than a decade running momentum strategies at hedge-fund scale, and the exponential-regression-times-R-squared metric genuinely improves on raw return sorting by stripping out gap-driven artifacts that inflate cruder screens. The improvement is real. It is also fully described, down to the parameter values, in a book anyone can purchase and implement in an afternoon with a price feed and a scripting language. Every stock that ranks highest under this metric will also rank highly under most other momentum screens, because a smooth uptrend visible to an exponential regression is visible to a twelve-month trailing return, to a moving-average crossover, to a pair of eyes watching a chart. The filtering catches the false positives that cruder methods miss, and that filtering is where whatever residual advantage sits. The hundred-day moving-average gate and the fifteen-percent gap disqualifier are sensible thresholds, but they are not derived from first principles; reasonable alternatives would produce similar rankings. What would distinguish this from a commodity screen is a filter calibrated to information nobody else has, and the published parameters are the opposite of that.

**What it takes.** The S&P 500 universe, ninety days of daily price data per stock, and the arithmetic to fit an exponential regression and multiply its annualized slope by R-squared. Published in full by Clenow, replicable in an afternoon by anyone with a price feed. Two filters gate entry: stocks below the hundred-day moving average are excluded, and any name that moved more than fifteen percent on a single event in the lookback window is disqualified. The ranking is the commodity part. The edge, if any persists, sits in the filtering that removes gap-distorted signals before they enter the portfolio, not in the ranking that every other momentum screen also produces.

## Reading Relative Strength by Hand

Drop the exponential regression. Drop R-squared. Drop the S&P 500 universe and the algorithmic ranking. A practitioner describes his primary filter as relative strength or relative weakness against the market, assessed visually rather than computed.<sup>[3]</sup> No formula enters the process at any point.

The method works inside consolidation zones where a stock has traded between defined support and resistance for days or weeks. When a name in that range holds firm on days the broad market falls, or advances faster than the index on days both rise, the bias shifts to long. When it drops on days the market advances, or drops harder than the index on days both decline, the bias shifts to short. You are reading one stock against the tape and letting the divergence tell you which side to take.

Entry sits at the range boundary. Relative strength confirmed, you buy the break above resistance. Relative weakness confirmed, you short the break below support. Position size is calibrated to the stock's recent average true range. Hold for up to three days, take bulk profits on continuation, and move on to the next name on the watchlist.

Primitive next to Clenow's regression. Deliberately so. The universe is a handful of names on a weekly watchlist, not five hundred stocks ranked by algorithm. The toolset is a chart and a pair of eyes.

But watch what the two approaches share. Both use relative strength as the primary signal. Both compare a stock's behavior against a broader benchmark. Both look for persistence rather than a one-session spike. Both treat the momentum concept as the core thesis and differ only in how they measure it and how many names they process. Clenow's system scales across five hundred names on a rebalancing schedule. The discretionary version processes a handful and adjusts in real time, aborting a trade mid-session if the character of relative strength changes intraday, something no periodic-rebalancing system can do.

The systematic version scales. The discretionary version does not. And the discretionary version may carry more edge precisely because it cannot scale.

**Reconstruction.** An anonymized practitioner, a self-reported video, no backtest, no documented returns, no sample size. On the evidence ladder that is a bottom rung, and stating it bluntly costs nothing. What keeps this case from sliding into anecdote is the structural rhyme with the section immediately above it. The same observation, that stocks strong relative to the market tend to stay strong in the near term, appears in an institutional systematic context with published parameters and in a discretionary swing-trading context with none, and both implementations rest on it as their entire thesis. If the underlying signal were fragile or idiosyncratic, the translation across those two wildly different execution substrates would break it. It has not broken. That does not validate either implementation; it suggests the observation itself is robust enough to survive being implemented badly, which is a different and weaker claim than saying either version produces durable returns. The discretionary wrapper adds judgment and real-time flexibility at the cost of inconsistency, emotional interference, and an inability to distinguish skill from luck over any sample the practitioner can reasonably accumulate. What would refute even the observation is a sustained period in which stocks showing relative strength systematically reversed within the holding window, and neither this source nor the academic momentum literature produces that counterexample, which is conspicuous enough to note rather than reassuring enough to rely on.

**What it takes.** No backtest, no documented returns, no track record, and no way to separate skill from luck over the sample this practitioner provides. A small watchlist of names tracked visually against the broad market, with entries at the range boundary when relative strength or weakness confirms the direction. Position size is calibrated to average true range, and the hold period runs up to three days before taking bulk profits. The flexibility to abort intraday is the one structural advantage over a weekly-rebalancing system, and it requires the screen time to exercise it. The signal is the most widely observed concept in technical analysis, which means the edge, if it exists, lives entirely in the discretionary execution that cannot scale.

## The Discipline of Waiting

Palantir in 2025 looked like the gift every short seller waits for. Extended run, moving averages converging underneath, the chart stretched far enough above any reasonable mean that the word "overextended" barely covered it.<sup>[4]</sup> A trader at a professional training firm watched it happen from his desk and did the one thing almost nobody in trading education content bothers to document. He passed.

Not because the analysis was wrong. The stock was extended and every momentum metric confirmed it. The mean-reversion case was right there on the screen, obvious, practically waving, and the chart pattern alone would have been enough to convince a less careful trader that the short was a gift. He refused to touch it.

His checklist carried three conditions, all required simultaneously before a short became actionable. Major volume expansion on the sell side, confirming that institutional participants were distributing rather than retail selling into strength. Significant extension from the moving averages, confirming that the character of the uptrend had broken rather than merely paused. Visible relative weakness against the broader market, confirming the problem was specific to this name and not a broad pullback about to reverse and carry everything higher.

Zero of three present. Sell-side volume had not expanded. The range had not broken. Relative weakness had not appeared. The stock sat far above its averages and its trend character was intact. Extended is not broken. The distinction costs money when you ignore it.

His conclusion was no position. Wait. Refuse the trade until every condition arrives at once, because a low-probability short on an extended stock inside a confirmed uptrend is the precise category of trade that bleeds capital when the premium is thin. A ten-percent annual premium absorbs the occasional wrong call. You take the marginal trade, lose on it, collect the next setup, and the math still works. Two percent does not absorb anything. Every trade that fails to meet full criteria takes a larger share of the annual return than it would have when the return was five times higher. Selectivity at two percent is not a preference. It is the entire operating model.

**Reconstruction.** The Palantir non-trade, reduced to its moving parts, is a three-input gate wired to a single output: no. Volume expansion, range break, relative weakness, all simultaneous, or the position does not exist. That is not a personality trait dressed as a method. It is a mechanical filter applied by a human, and what makes it interesting is not the filter itself but what the filter refuses. The source is an anonymized trader presenting a weekly plan in a video. No backtest accompanies the decision. No P&L is reported. Whether the three conditions eventually arrived and produced a profitable short are questions the source does not revisit. The entire evidentiary basis is a described non-event, bottom rung of the evidence ladder, and stating it that way costs nothing. What keeps the case structurally useful is the rarity of the specimen. Trading education content selects for action, for the trade that paid or the trade that taught something. A trade that never happened generates neither a return nor a narrative, and the incentive to omit it from a weekly video is strong. This practitioner publishes it because in a compressed premium the capital preserved by refusing one marginal setup compounds into the next setup that meets full criteria. What would prove the thesis wrong is a demonstration that marginal trades in a two-percent premium carry positive expectancy. The cross-sectional momentum literature does not supply one.

**What it takes.** You are watching Palantir in 2025, extended by every momentum metric, and the correct position is no position. Three conditions before a short becomes actionable: major volume expansion on the sell side, significant extension from moving averages confirming the uptrend's character has changed, and visible relative weakness against the broader market. All three must arrive simultaneously. The premium has compressed from roughly ten percent to two, which means the cost of a marginal short on an extended stock in a confirmed uptrend is proportionally larger than the reward of a correct one. The checklist does not find trades. It refuses them until the configuration where historical edge is highest appears, and the outcome on Palantir is not reported.

## What a Shrinking Premium Teaches

Four implementations of the same signal, and every one of them lands on the same constraint. The factor regression runs published coefficients through a hundred daily positions and discovers that the edge has migrated from the factors to the execution cost. Clenow's slope-times-R-squared strips out the gap-driven noise and discovers that a cleaner ranking still ranks the same crowded names. The discretionary relative-strength trader reads the signal by hand from a handful of stocks and discovers that flexibility compensates for rigor only when the premium is wide enough to tolerate inconsistency. The Palantir non-trade discovers that in a two-percent environment the decision not to act may be worth more than the decision to act on anything less than a full setup.

The trajectory underneath all four is the same compression documented at the top of this chapter. Ten percent to roughly two percent, and the mechanism is not obscure. The cross-sectional momentum premium was a behavioral inefficiency: investors underreacting to relative strength, continuing to hold losers and refusing to buy winners. It persisted because not enough capital was positioned to exploit it. The research identified it. The machines arrived. The arbitrage did not kill the edge. It taxed it, steadily, and two percent is what remains after the tax.

Each layer of refinement you add on top of the raw signal is an attempt to survive inside that two-percent world. Academic factors are public. The exponential regression is published. Relative strength is the most widely observed concept in technical analysis. Nothing about the signal is proprietary. What varies across the four cases is the filter applied after the signal fires, the execution quality behind the filter, and the willingness to refuse the trade when the filter says wait.

That migration tells you something about edges in general. When a signal goes public and enough capital agrees that it works, the source of whatever return persists shifts from information to implementation and finally to discipline. The ranking becomes the commodity. The execution becomes the differentiator. The willingness to sit with no position, watching the obvious trade go by because your conditions have not arrived, becomes the last edge standing.

Cross-sectional momentum sits at a boundary the previous chapters did not reach. Breakout patterns depend on how crowds behave at price levels. Trend-following depends on how crowds underreact to sustained directional moves. Both edges are behavioral in origin and behavioral in implementation. The momentum premium shares the behavioral root, investors underreacting to relative strength, but its implementation is algorithmic, and the compression arrived through that algorithmic channel. An edge with a behavioral origin and a mechanical harvesting mechanism gets the worst of both paths: the machines compress it while the behavioral persistence underneath supplies just enough signal to keep new capital arriving, each entrant compressing the premium a little further, none of them the last.

## Key Points

- Cross-sectional momentum has lost the large majority of its original premium as arbitrage capital piled in, a textbook case of publication-driven decay.
- Academic factor models and proprietary ranking systems exploit the same underlying signal; the factors are public, so the edge has migrated from information to execution.
- Clenow's slope-times-R-squared penalizes gap-driven false momentum, but the improvement refines a crowded signal rather than discovering a new one.
- Discretionary relative-strength trading applies the same principle at smaller scale, gaining flexibility but sacrificing statistical rigor and scalability.
- In a thin-premium environment, the discipline to refuse marginal trades may matter more than the signal that identifies them.

Some price moves have nothing to do with crowd behavior. They are caused by specific events: an earnings report that reshapes a company's valuation, a crisis that forces liquidation, a merger that creates a pricing arbitrage, a macro headline that repositions an entire sector in a single session. Where momentum captures what the market failed to price last month, event catalysts force pricing in real time, compressing the lag from weeks to hours or minutes. One is a memory trade. The other is a forcing function. The next chapter follows those forcing functions.
