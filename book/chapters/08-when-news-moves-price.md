# Chapter 8: When News Moves Price

*A crisis, an earnings report, a management statement that changes tone mid-sentence. Each one forces participants to revalue positions on a schedule they did not choose. The edge that follows is real, episodic, and gone the moment the forcing function exhausts itself. You trade the event or you trade nothing, and between events the capital sits idle.*

## The Panic Phase

Thirty pounds per point on a spread bet against the FTSE 100, and a position in 3UKS, the triple-leveraged short ETF, opened while the index was still within sight of its highs. Early 2020. A respiratory virus was spreading through Wuhan, then Italy, then everywhere. Most traders in UK equities watched the same headlines. Robbie Burns, the self-styled Naked Trader, had already moved. He cut his long positions by roughly half, converting 30 to 50 percent of his portfolio to cash. Then he built the short.<sup>[1]</sup>

The trailing stop sat 300 points away from the current level. That is roughly five percent of the index value, a buffer so wide that ordinary market noise cannot touch it. As the index fell, the stop followed it down, always keeping at least that 300-point cushion. Once profit accumulated and the stop reached breakeven, the trade changed character. Further downside was captured. A reversal could no longer produce a loss. You are holding a position that can only pay or expire flat, and you paid for that asymmetry with nothing except the willingness to set the stop wide enough to survive the intraday spikes that destroy tighter levels during genuine panics.

The exit rule was blunt. Close the shorts when the market stops falling on consecutive days and recovery signals appear. Burns reports profits exceeding 220,000 pounds from these positions, documented with profit screenshots in the source material.<sup>[1]</sup>

A triple-leveraged short ETF compounds daily. Hold it through a multi-week decline and the arithmetic diverges from a simple short position. On days when the market bounces, the ETF loses value disproportionately, even if the underlying index merely returns to its prior level. Over weeks, compounding drag erodes returns compared to what a simple short would have produced. The trailing stop rule, set wide enough to survive noise, is what makes the trade survivable in the face of that compounding problem. Without it, the position bleeds on every counter-trend day and the holder has no structural reason to stay.

**Reconstruction.** A pandemic is not a chart pattern. It does not form on a screen. It forces participants to sell not because they want to but because their risk models demand it, their clients redeem, their margin departments send letters. The selling is mechanical, directional, and persistent for a defined period, and that period is the trade.

How long does the period last? In the Covid sell-off, the FTSE 100 lost roughly a third of its value in weeks, then recovered most of the decline within months. Burns's trailing stop rode the entire downward move and exited as the reversal began. Not because he predicted the pandemic. Because the stop was designed for exactly this shape of event.

The cold question is frequency. Black-swan shorting is alive as an edge family. But genuine crises arrive at irregular intervals. Twelve years separated the 2008 financial crisis from the 2020 pandemic. A strategy that fires once per decade is a contingency plan, not a strategy in the conventional sense. The capital and the plan sit idle between events. And without the trailing stop buffer, the position is a coin flip dressed as conviction.

What would show this wrong is a crisis where the decline persists long enough for the leveraged ETF's daily compounding to destroy the position before the stop catches it. That scenario has not been documented here. It has also not been ruled out.

**What it takes.** A UK brokerage account supporting spread bets at ten to fifty pounds per point and access to 3UKS or an equivalent triple-leveraged short instrument. Capital sized for a 300-point trailing stop on the FTSE 100, roughly five percent of the index. The compounding drag on the leveraged ETF erodes returns on every bounce, so a multi-week hold delivers less than a simple short. Twelve years separated the two comparable events in the record. Between events, the capital and the plan sit idle, and idle capital earning nothing is the cost of the contingency.

## Buying the Wreckage

Sectors beaten down 50 to 70 percent from pre-crisis peaks, but only after the crisis itself has begun to resolve. Burns describes the mirror trade, and its conditions are specific enough to separate it from vague optimism.<sup>[2]</sup>

Four criteria must hold simultaneously. The company carries net cash or manageable debt relative to its cash flow, because a company that survived the crisis financially has a baseline for recovery that a leveraged company does not. Demand indicators are turning upward: bookings rising, reopenings confirmed, capacity returning. The fundamental business model is intact, and only sentiment has collapsed, which is the entire premise of the trade and the reason the discount exists in the first place. And the sectors Burns names as candidates are those most directly affected by the crisis and most directly benefiting from its resolution: travel, hospitality, and discretionary retail.<sup>[2]</sup>

The target is a 50 to 60 percent recovery from the trough toward pre-crisis levels. Not a full recovery. Not even close. Burns is not predicting a return to the old highs. He is predicting a partial normalization, roughly half the decline recovered, on the premise that a business whose model was sound before the crisis will be sound again once the crisis passes. The first half of the recovery reprices faster because it is event-driven. The second half, if it comes at all, takes much longer and involves fundamentals that nobody is in a hurry to reassess.

The stop sits 10 to 15 percent below entry, and the position gets cut if new crisis signals emerge. Burns reports profits of two to three thousand pounds per trade from these positions.<sup>[2]</sup> Modest numbers. Consistent with the position sizes of a retail UK trader working in FTSE-listed equities. But look at the ratio. A 50 percent recovery from a 60 percent decline is roughly a 30 percent gain from the entry point. Against a 10 to 15 percent stop, you are holding reward-to-risk of approximately 2 to 3.<sup>[2]</sup>

**Reconstruction.** Panic selling overshoots, and it must overshoot, because the sellers are not calibrating to value. They are selling to meet redemptions, to satisfy margin requirements, to reduce exposure by mandate. The mechanism is contractual, not discretionary, and it runs until the contracts say stop. When the mandate relaxes, the overshoot corrects.

A sector that fell 50 percent on panic rather than fundamental deterioration carries a higher base rate for partial recovery than any random sector at any time. The reason is structural: the forcing function reversed. Forced selling created the decline, and the absence of forced selling allows the price to float back toward something closer to intrinsic value. You do not need to know where intrinsic value sits. You need to know the forcing function has changed direction.

A value investor buys cheapness as a permanent analytical lens, screening at any point in the cycle. An event-driven trader buys the resolution of a specific forcing function. The crisis created the dislocation. The resolution removes it. The edge sits in the transition between those two states, and it has a time limit. Once the recovery is priced in, the edge is gone. That window closes. Holding past that point is a different trade with a different thesis.

What would distinguish this from a value trap is a sector where the business model broke during the crisis rather than merely losing revenue. Travel recovered. Physical retail, in many cases, did not.

**What it takes.** If you cannot screen for net-cash companies in travel, hospitality, and discretionary retail after the crisis has begun to resolve, the trade does not start. Capital that absorbs a 10 to 15 percent stop on multiple positions simultaneously. The target is roughly a 30 percent gain from entry for reward-to-risk of about 2 to 3. What disqualifies the trade is entry before the forcing function reverses. Buying while the mandate to sell is still active produces a loss, not a discount.

## Reading the Narrative Shift

Revenue guidance moves from vague to specific in a single quarterly statement, and nobody on the sell side updates for three weeks. That gap is the trade.

Burns describes a process for detecting narrative shifts before the market fully prices them, and the signal sources are layered rather than singular.<sup>[3]</sup> A momentum screen on Stockopedia flags the initial pickup: the stock is appearing on screens where it has not appeared before, or its ranking is changing in a direction that suggests institutional accumulation. A close reading of the company's latest trading statement confirms the direction. Sector sentiment provides the backdrop. No single signal is sufficient. The layering itself is a form of risk management.

The statement reading is where the real work sits. Management's language shifts in detectable ways, but detection requires your attention to nuance rather than keyword scanning. Revenue guidance moves from vague to specific. Management commentary shifts from defensive to assertive. New product lines appear with timelines rather than aspirations. Not quantitative signals. Linguistic ones, and they carry information that the market processes slowly because different investors read with different levels of care, and most of them are not reading the statement at all but waiting for an analyst to summarize it in a note that arrives days later.<sup>[3]</sup>

For a positive story change, Burns targets a 30 to 50 percent gain. The stop sits 15 percent below entry for long positions. For a negative story change, detected through the opposite signals, you short with a 10 percent stop above entry.<sup>[3]</sup>

**Reconstruction.** Markets re-rate as narratives change. The re-rating takes time because different classes of investors update at different speeds. Retail investors react to headlines and price movements. Institutional analysts update their models after reading the full statement, adjusting revenue and earnings forecasts over days. Fund managers adjust their positions at the next portfolio review meeting. Each layer processes the same information on a different timeline.

The edge, if it exists, sits in the gap between the narrative shift and the full re-rating. The shift is detectable in the trading statement. The re-rating is complete only after the final layer of institutional processing. That gap can last weeks or months.

Durability depends on a constraint that protects it. An algorithm scanning for sentiment keywords in press releases captures a fraction of the signal. The rest lives in context, tone, and what is left unsaid. A company that removes the phrase "challenging conditions" from its outlook statement sends a different signal than one that adds "better than expected." Both are positive. They carry different weights. The market may react differently to each. That kind of nuance cannot be fully automated, which means it cannot be fully arbitraged, which means the edge has a capacity limit that quantitative edges do not.

What would show this is not an edge is a market where corporate communications become so standardized that narrative shifts vanish into boilerplate. That has not happened. The variation between companies has increased.

**What it takes.** Three signal sources running in parallel: a momentum screen flagging new appearances, a close read of the latest trading statement, and a sector sentiment backdrop. A brokerage account supporting positions with a 15 percent stop for longs and 10 percent for shorts. The work is in the statement reading, and it does not scale. Revenue guidance moving from vague to specific, management tone shifting from defensive to assertive, product timelines replacing aspirations. None of that registers on a keyword scan. An algorithm captures a fraction of the signal. The rest sits in what the company stopped saying, and reading that takes someone who has read enough statements to know what normal looks like.

## Buy the Rumor, Sell the News

Twenty percent above where it sat a few weeks ago, and the earnings date is circled on the calendar. That is the setup Burns isolates.<sup>[4]</sup> A stock that has already climbed 20 percent or more heading into a results announcement carries confirmation, not surprise. The move tells you the news is baked in. His rule: exit one to two days before the number drops.

The logic is mechanical and very old. Traders who bought on anticipation of good news take profits once the news confirms their thesis. You bought because you expected good numbers. The numbers arrive. They are good. Now what? The catalyst that justified the position has been consumed. Holding through the announcement carries the risk that the next catalyst is worse, and there is no next catalyst on the calendar for another quarter. So you sell. Not because the company disappointed. Because it delivered exactly what you already owned.

This creates a predictable dip of 5 to 15 percent in the days after the announcement, as profit-taking overwhelms the modest incremental buying from investors who waited for confirmation. The re-entry comes when the profit-taking exhausts itself and the price stabilizes.<sup>[4]</sup>

**Reconstruction.** Post-earnings announcement drift is one of the most replicated anomalies in financial economics: stocks continue moving in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeks or months after the announcement. The buy-rumor-sell-news pattern trades a different margin entirely. It does not trade the surprise. It trades the transition from anticipation to confirmation.

Those are two different trades on the same calendar date. The drift trader buys after the surprise and holds. The buy-rumor-sell-news trader sells before the confirmation and buys back lower. Both can be right simultaneously. They are operating on different time horizons against different counterparties.

The pattern is alive but crowded. Every institutional desk runs some version of pre-announcement drift analysis, calibrated to the magnitude of the pre-announcement run. The 20 percent threshold is a heuristic, not a derived optimum. A stock that rose 18 percent may or may not exhibit the same pullback. A stock that rose 25 percent may exhibit a larger one. The threshold is a practical filter. Not a natural law. But the underlying mechanism, that confirmation of expected good news triggers profit-taking, is behavioral and therefore difficult to arbitrage away entirely. People take profits. The question is whether the magnitude of the post-confirmation dip pays for the risk that occasionally the news surprises to the upside and the stock gaps further, leaving you behind.

**What it takes.** A screen that tracks holdings against their announcement calendar and flags those already up 20 percent or more. Sell one to two days ahead of the statement and re-enter after the 5 to 15 percent dip. The 20 percent threshold is a heuristic, not a measured optimum. Every institutional desk runs its own version, and the pullback you are capturing is the same one the desk beside you is capturing. Competition compresses the dip.

## The Power Earnings Gap

Four filters, applied in sequence on the morning the number drops, and each one eliminates a different species of false positive. A professional trader with over twenty years of experience describes a specific pattern he calls the Power Earnings Gap, and the definition is precise.<sup>[5]</sup>

The stock reports strong earnings. It gaps up on the open. It closes near the highs of the day, not red. And it does so on volume at least two to three times the average daily volume. Each filter does specific work. The gap up establishes that the market is surprised: this was not priced in. The close near the highs shows that early sellers were absorbed and that buying pressure dominated the entire session, not just the opening print but the full six and a half hours from the bell to the close, with every wave of profit-taking absorbed by fresh demand that kept the price pinned near its high. A stock that gaps up but closes near its lows has been sold into by institutions. Opposite signal. And volume at two to three times the average is the signature of institutional participation. Retail traders do not move volume to that degree on a single session. When volume hits that multiple on an earnings gap, large holders are entering or adding, not merely reacting to headlines.<sup>[5]</sup>

Short interest is the amplifier. If it exceeds 10 percent of the float, the Power Earnings Gap becomes a more explosive setup. Short sellers who were betting against the stock are now trapped on the wrong side of a gap, watching the position move against them tick by tick while the margin department recalculates their exposure in real time. Their forced covering adds buying pressure to an already strong move, and the covering pushes the price higher, which forces more shorts to cover, which pushes it higher still. Arithmetic, not opinion. The 10 percent threshold is the level at which this forced-covering dynamic becomes material enough to amplify the initial move.<sup>[5]</sup>

But you do not buy the gap day. That is what separates the Power Earnings Gap from a crude momentum chase. The trader waits for the smart pullback: a slow, low-volume, multi-day retreat to the 20-day moving average. Three characteristics define it. Gradual decline, not sharp. Volume contracts. The move to the 20-day average takes days, not hours.<sup>[5]</sup>

Those conditions diagnose ownership. A sharp pullback on heavy volume suggests that the institutions who bought the gap are now selling. Distribution. Over. A gradual pullback on declining volume suggests the opposite: institutions are holding, and the selling comes from weaker hands who bought the gap for a quick trade and are now exiting flat or taking small profits. You are watching a market that shakes off weak holders while strong ones remain. Consolidation. And consolidation after an earnings gap on institutional volume is the setup.

**Reconstruction.** Six filters stacked on one question: did institutions commit real capital to this stock on the basis of new fundamental information? The earnings surprise provides the information. The gap provides the commitment signal. Volume provides scale. The close near highs provides persistence. Short interest adds a mechanical amplifier. The smart pullback provides re-entry timing.

Each layer removes a category of false positive. What survives all six is a stock where the probability of continued upside is meaningfully higher than the base rate. That probability is not a number you can look up. It is the residue of elimination.

Earnings drift is alive as a phenomenon, documented across decades of academic research across multiple markets. The pattern survives because of a structural feature in how information is processed: institutional investors update positions on different timescales, and the full repricing of an earnings surprise takes longer than the immediate reaction. As more capital deploys specifically to capture post-earnings drift, returns compress. The filters that define the Power Earnings Gap thin the eligible universe, which simultaneously reduces competition and reduces how often the setup appears. What would prove the filters insufficient is a market where the gap-day volume signal stops distinguishing institutional entry from algorithmic noise. That distinction is getting harder to draw.

**What it takes.** Volume data you can read in real time and a scanner that flags earnings gaps on the morning they happen. A stock that gaps up on two-to-three-times average volume, closes near the highs, then pulls back gradually on declining volume to the 20-day moving average over multiple days. Short interest above 10 percent of the float adds the forced-covering amplifier. Capital that stays allocated through a multi-day pullback while the thesis sits unconfirmed. Strict filters mean fewer qualifying names per quarter and long stretches of idle capital between setups.

## First Profitable Quarter

A company that has never made money files a quarterly report showing positive earnings for the first time. Look at what happens next. The stock crosses a line that most investors never think about but that governs where billions of dollars are allowed to go. A professional trading desk uses this pattern, targeting companies that report their first profitable quarter after a history of losses.<sup>[6]</sup>

The catalyst is not the magnitude of the earnings surprise. It is the fact of profitability itself. A company reporting its first positive quarter moves from one category to another: from speculative, loss-making, funded by hope and dilution, to viable, cash-generating, investable by mandate-constrained funds. Yesterday you could not own it. Today you can.

That category shift is the edge. Many institutional mandates prohibit or discourage investment in companies with negative earnings. A pension fund with a quality screen. A dividend-oriented ETF with a profitability filter. A long-only equity fund with a "no loss-makers" rule. All of these mandates exclude the company before its first profitable quarter, and they exclude it not because any analyst looked at the business and rejected it but because the mandate's profitability filter threw it out before a human ever saw the name. When the company crosses the profitability line, it becomes eligible for a larger pool of capital, and the transition from speculation to proof attracts money that was structurally excluded before. That money arrives on a schedule dictated by the mandates rather than by market sentiment.

The desk enters on day one of the earnings report, at the opening or in early action following the announcement. Fastly is cited as a stock that the desk caught on this pattern.<sup>[6]</sup>

The cold assessment requires care. Self-reported edge. Commercial source. No backtested data accompanies the claim. No documented returns. No sample size. No win rate. The underlying logic is plausible: profitability inflection points attract institutional capital, and institutional analysts publish "initiating coverage" notes when previously uncovered companies cross thresholds that make them eligible for coverage. First profitability is one of those thresholds. But the specific claim that day-one entry on first profitable quarters generates consistent returns is a practitioner assertion without quantitative support. The pattern exists as a category of event. The edge is unquantified. It may be real, and the logic supports it, but the evidence does not confirm it.

## The Episodic Pivot

LITE, a fiber optics company, files a quarter where earnings-per-share growth and revenue growth both accelerate dramatically compared to the prior period. Not just profitable. Accelerating. The same desk that trades first profitable quarters describes this as the second-order pattern: the acceleration inflection quarter.<sup>[7]</sup>

They call it an episodic pivot. One quarter of positive earnings proves the model can make money. Two quarters, with the second accelerating past the first, proves something harder to dismiss: the company is not stumbling into profitability but climbing a slope that steepens as it goes. The arithmetic sharpens the point. Earn $0.05 per share one quarter, $0.15 the next. That is a triple in a single period. Earn $1.00 and then $1.10. That is 10 percent growth, respectable in a mature business but unremarkable in a company that just crossed the break-even line. The absolute numbers may favor the second company. The trajectory favors the first. And for a business that was hemorrhaging cash six months ago, trajectory is the thing that separates a viable operation from a growth story.<sup>[7]</sup>

You enter the same way as the first-profitable-quarter pattern: day one of the earnings announcement, with particular attention to follow-through in the first and second weeks after the report. LITE was flagged when it reported an acceleration quarter in the context of a broader sector theme in fiber optics.<sup>[7]</sup>

The same cold caveat applies. Practitioner framework. Self-reported. Commercial source. The underlying logic is well grounded in how fundamental analysis works. But the specific edge is asserted rather than demonstrated.

**Reconstruction.** The first profitable quarter and the acceleration quarter sit at different points on the same institutional processing chain, and the chain is what creates the drift. Quantitative screens detect the profitability change within hours. Fundamental analysts update their models within days. Portfolio managers adjust allocations at the next review meeting. Fund mandates are reviewed quarterly.

Four layers of institutional processing, four different speeds, and the price adjustment spreads across all of them. That staggered response is the structural basis of post-earnings drift. It is also why event-driven edges around earnings announcements persist despite being widely known. Everyone knows that earnings surprises move stocks. Knowing it does not compress it, because the processing chain is not one decision maker. It is thousands of decision makers operating on mandated schedules they cannot accelerate.

The acceleration quarter adds a specific signal to this chain: the inflection was real. A single profitable quarter can be accounting. Two quarters of accelerating growth is harder to dismiss as timing. The threshold crossed is not just profitability. It is trajectory, and trajectory is what triggers the fund mandates that respond to growth rather than to viability. What would prove the logic wrong is an acceleration quarter that consistently fails to produce drift, and the practitioner source offers no data on that question.

**What it takes.** No backtest supports the specific claim, and no win rate has been documented. An earnings scanner that flags first-profitable-quarter companies and then monitors the follow-on quarter for acceleration in both EPS and revenue growth rates. Day-one entry at the opening or in early action. The edge is asserted by a commercial source, not demonstrated in data, so every position is underwritten by the logic of mandate-driven capital flows rather than by measured returns.

## When the Headline Hits

Tariffs on Chinese exports and export controls on critical software, announced in one session, and the broad market dropped 3 percent intraday. Nineteen billion dollars in leveraged crypto positions liquidated simultaneously as margin systems force-closed Bitcoin and altcoin accounts across exchanges. One desk reported profits in the seven figures on that single session.<sup>[8]</sup>

Professionals call it a trend day: a session where the market picks a direction in the first hour and holds it through the close with minimal counter-trend action, high volume, and clear directional conviction that does not waver from the opening print to the final tick. Most sessions do not behave this way. Most sessions are range-bound, the price oscillating around a central level, giving back gains and losses in alternating waves until the day's range looks like noise. On a trend day, the oscillation vanishes. Direction is set early. The market does not look back.

You can see it in the first thirty minutes. The opening move is large. The first pullback is shallow and brief. Volume runs abnormally high. Price does not return to the opening range.<sup>[8]</sup> Those four features appear before most participants have finished reading the headline. By the time a trader has parsed the tariff announcement, identified the affected sectors, and decided whether to act, the trend day is half over.

The seven-figure profit number deserves its own scrutiny. It is a reported figure from a commercial source, not documented with trade logs or account statements. Consistent with the position sizes available to a professional prop desk on a 3 percent index move, yes. Also the kind of headline number that attracts viewers to a promotional video. The mechanism is sound. The specific figure is self-reported.

**Reconstruction.** Nineteen billion dollars in crypto liquidations did not happen because traders panicked. It happened because their margin systems executed. Risk models recalculated exposure limits. Margin departments issued calls across thousands of accounts. Automated portfolio adjustments fired according to pre-set rules. Every algorithm trained on the same volatility triggers sent the same orders to the same exchanges within the same minute. The cascade that followed was infrastructure responding to a trigger, and infrastructure does not hesitate.

That distinction matters. A crowd selling in fear can reverse when the fear subsides. A cascade driven by contractual obligations runs until the obligations are satisfied. The selling stops when the margin calls are met, not when sentiment improves.

The edge is episodic. No one manufactures a macro catalyst. You recognize one when it arrives or you do not. The desk that reported seven figures had capital deployed, risk limits pre-set, and recognition capability fast enough to commit within the first hour. A retail trader without that infrastructure enters later, at a worse fill, for a smaller position, and at a point where much of the daily range has already printed. The same event produced two entirely different trades depending on who was sitting in front of the screen when the headline crossed.

What would show this framework wrong is a macro catalyst that reverses intraday, trapping the trend-day trader on the wrong side. That risk is real and undocumented here.

**What it takes.** If you cannot commit capital within the first hour of a macro catalyst, the trend day trades without you. Recognition speed, not directional opinion, is the binding constraint. A large opening move, shallow first pullback, abnormally high volume, and price that does not revisit the opening range are the diagnostic features, all visible within thirty minutes. Capital pre-deployed with risk limits already set. Real-time data feeds and execution infrastructure that turns a read into an order before the morning is over. A retail trader entering after that window is buying the tail of a move that institutional desks captured at the body.

## The Merger Spread

Company A announces it will acquire Company B at $50 per share. Company B trades at $48. An arbitrageur buys Company B and collects the $2 spread when the deal closes. That is the entire trade.<sup>[9]</sup>

Two dollars per share. The oldest event-driven strategy in the institutional playbook, and you can explain the whole thing to someone who has never opened a brokerage account.

The spread exists because the deal might not close. Regulatory approval might be denied. Financing might fall through. A competing bidder might emerge. The acquirer might walk away. Each of those risks pushes the target's price below the offer, and the gap between the two is the compensation the arbitrageur collects for bearing uncertainty during the months between announcement and close. Wider spreads signal higher perceived risk. Tighter spreads signal higher perceived probability of closure.<sup>[9]</sup>

An institutional merger arbitrage desk analyzes hundreds of variables per deal: antitrust risk, financing conditions, the track record of the acquirer, the political environment for the sector, the likelihood of a competing bid, the break fee provisions, the regulatory timeline. The $2 spread on a deal with 95 percent probability of closing produces a very different risk-adjusted return than the same $2 spread on a deal at 80 percent. You are not trading a number. You are trading a probability, and the quality of the probability assessment is the edge.

For a retail participant, the edge is real but thin. The spread between $48 and $50 on a deal that closes in six months represents roughly 4 percent, annualized to approximately 8 percent. Below most retail return targets, and the capital is locked in a position that carries binary outcome risk: the deal closes or it does not, and there is no middle ground.

**Reconstruction.** Merger arbitrage, described as a spread, sounds like a discount. Described accurately, it is an insurance contract. The arbitrageur sells insurance on deal completion. The $2 per share is the premium. Collect if the deal closes. If it breaks, the stock falls well below the pre-announcement price because the takeover premium evaporates, and the loss is many multiples of the spread collected.

That asymmetry explains why the strategy belongs to specialists. The premium is small. The claim is large. Every insurance underwriter in every industry faces this arithmetic, and they solve it the same way: diversification across uncorrelated risks. One deal breaks and the losses are absorbed by premiums collected on the dozens that closed. A retail trader running one or two merger positions has no portfolio. A single break wipes out years of collected spreads.

Merger spreads persist because the uncertainty is real and the analysis required to price it is labor-intensive. But the returns match the risk only at a scale where diversification operates. At retail scale, a six-month deal annualizing to roughly 8 percent sits below the threshold where the binary risk justifies the tied-up capital. The strategy works. It works for desks that run dozens of deals at once.

What would undermine this edge is a regulatory environment where deal approvals become predictable enough to compress spreads below the cost of analysis. That has not happened. Regulatory uncertainty has widened.

**What it takes.** Dozens of simultaneous deal positions to diversify the binary risk, and capital that holds through regulatory timelines running months. Antitrust analysis and financing-condition monitoring, deal by deal. The $2 spread on a deal at 95 percent close probability and the same spread at 80 percent are different trades entirely. At retail scale, one or two positions carry the full binary exposure with no portfolio to absorb a break. A six-month deal annualizing to roughly 8 percent sits below where the risk justifies the capital.

## The Scanner at the Gate

Half a million dollars of a CEO's personal money, placed into shares of the company the CEO runs, filed with the SEC where anyone can find it. An AI agent called Eddie queries that database every morning, filtered for exactly this signal: insider purchases by company CEOs at $500,000 or more.<sup>[10]</sup>

The logic behind it is information asymmetry compressed into a filing. Federal law requires corporate insiders to disclose their trades, and the disclosures are searchable in the SEC's public database. A CEO who puts half a million dollars of personal money into shares of the company is signaling something that you, scanning the same database, can read the morning after. The CEO knows the pipeline, the order book, the regulatory outlook, and the upcoming earnings trajectory. A large personal purchase suggests confidence that the public market has not yet priced.

Eddie scans the database daily, extracts the relevant filings, and passes the signal to a human team for evaluation. The agent is a filter, not a decision maker.<sup>[10]</sup>

The evidence behind the signal is bare assertion. No performance data. No backtest. No track record. No documented return sits behind the claim. Eddie surfaces the filing. A human decides whether it matters.

The academic evidence on insider-trading signals is extensive but inconclusive at the level of actionable edge. Some studies find that CEO purchases are followed by modest positive abnormal returns over subsequent months, particularly when the purchases are large relative to the insider's compensation and when they are clustered with multiple insiders buying simultaneously. Others find that the signal has been arbitraged as SEC filing requirements have tightened, shrinking the window of information asymmetry. The faster the filings become public, the faster the market prices the information, and the smaller the residual edge.

An AI agent querying the SEC database daily automates a process that any Bloomberg terminal user can already perform. The technology is a convenience. Not an edge. The edge, if it exists, sits upstream of the scan: in the analytical framework that distinguishes a signal-bearing CEO purchase from a noise purchase. If you see a new CEO buying into a turnaround thesis, or a founder increasing a position ahead of a known catalyst, that carries a different signal than a routine annual buy by a long-tenured CEO who purchases the same amount every January as part of a planned program.

**Reconstruction.** Multiply the scanner across every public information stream and the pattern becomes clear. Earnings transcripts analyzed by natural-language models for sentiment shifts. Patent filings monitored for competitive signals. FDA approval databases tracked for clinical-trial outcomes. Satellite imagery of retail parking lots estimating same-store sales before the quarterly report. Shipping data from port authorities estimating trade volumes before official statistics release. Each stream generates event signals that, in theory, arrive before the market fully prices them.

The question is simple. Does speed of detection translate to edge? A thousand scanners watching the same SEC database price the signal within minutes of filing. A hundred hedge funds analyzing the same satellite imagery close the parking-lot edge before a retail trader finishes downloading the images. The technology democratizes access. It simultaneously compresses the edge by accelerating the market's response.

What survives is not the scan. It is the filter behind the scan. Eddie flags a filing. The analyst decides whether the filing is a signal or a routine transaction. That judgment, the part that cannot be automated without losing the context that makes it valuable, is where the residual edge sits. A scan without a filter is a list. Not a trade. And a thousand lists converging on the same database produce a market that prices the obvious signals instantly, leaving only the ambiguous ones for anyone paying attention.

**What it takes.** You scan the SEC insider-transaction database daily for CEO purchases above $500,000 in personal funds. That is the minimum infrastructure and it is not the edge. Any Bloomberg terminal runs the same query. The edge sits in the analytical layer that distinguishes signal from noise: a new CEO buying a turnaround versus a long-tenured officer executing a planned annual purchase. Clustered insider purchases, multiple officers buying simultaneously, carry more signal than a single filing. The academic evidence reports modest abnormal returns following large insider purchases and also reports that the window has compressed as filings become public faster. Speed of scan is table stakes. Quality of filter is the constraint.

## Key Points

- Event-driven edges require a forcing function, some catalyst that compels participants to act regardless of whether they want to.
- The strongest event signals combine multiple confirmations: volume, short interest, narrative shift, or institutional flow alongside the catalyst itself.
- Crisis shorting and post-crisis recovery are mirror trades, both governed by the same forcing function operating in opposite directions.
- Earnings patterns like the Power Earnings Gap layer filters to identify institutional conviction, separating real capital commitment from reactive noise.
- Merger arbitrage and insider-signal scanning are alive but capacity-constrained, with returns that compress as more capital competes for the same spreads.

AI-driven event scanning represents the frontier of event detection. But events, even when tradeable, share a common limitation: they are episodic. You wait for the catalyst, and between catalysts, the capital sits idle. The next part of this book turns to edges that do not wait for events at all. These are edges embedded in the structure of markets themselves, where the forcing function is not a headline but a mandate, and the flow is not episodic but continuous.
