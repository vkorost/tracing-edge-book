# Chapter 14: What Survives

*Twelve chapters of evidence converge on a single question. Edges were traced from discovery through exploitation, crowding, and decay. The durability verdicts now land in a pattern you can read: the edges that survive are the ones whose counterparties cannot stop losing, and the traders who survive are the ones who size for the regime they cannot predict.*

---

## The Copying Machine

Ninety-seven documented stock-return predictors walked into the post-publication record, and fifty-eight percent of their alpha did not walk out.

R. David McLean and Jeffrey Pontiff published that autopsy in 2016. They tracked each predictor from its original sample period through the years after the paper appeared in an academic journal.<sup>[1]</sup> Twenty-six percentage points of the decline turned out to be statistical artifact, the inevitable shrinkage when a pattern discovered through data mining meets fresh data. The remaining thirty-two points were competition. Quantitative funds read the papers. Statistical arbitrageurs implemented the signals. Increasingly automated strategy builders pushed prices toward the efficient level before slower capital could act. A five percent in-sample alpha became, on average, roughly 2.1 percent after publication. Not zero. But a fraction of what the discoverer measured, and the clock started ticking before the journal went to press.

Three subsequent studies tighten the screws. Linnainmaa and Roberts went back furthest, testing thirty-six factors against pre-sample data extending to 1926, and roughly sixty percent of the alphas that looked significant in the discovery period turned out to be insignificant in the prior era. Data snooping, not genuine signal, accounted for a substantial fraction of what researchers originally reported.<sup>[4]</sup> Jacobs and Muller widened the lens to 241 anomalies across thirty-nine markets and landed on an uncomfortable geography lesson: the decay is largely a U.S. phenomenon.<sup>[3]</sup> Capital constraints, short-selling restrictions, and less sophisticated institutional participation still shield anomaly returns in other countries. Kaplanski, studying seventy-one anomalies in 2023, found that the compression is arriving earlier now, closer to or before formal publication, because practitioners monitor SSRN preprints rather than waiting for peer review.<sup>[2]</sup> If you are tracking the decay clock, stop looking at journal dates. The posting date, or perhaps the citation count, is the trigger that matters.

Step back and read this as a practitioner rather than a researcher. Regulators did not compete the edge away. The original discoverers did not compete it away. The participants on the other side of the trade from slower money did. Publication is a broadcast: it hands the signal to everyone capable of harvesting it, and the harvesting starts before the ink dries. You already know a documented edge will decay. The only operational questions are how fast and whether anything is left once the copying machine finishes its run.

Not all edges decay at the same speed, and the evidence on what separates the fast deaths from the slow ones is consistent. Hyperbolic decay hits signals that are mechanical, unambiguous, and cheap to replicate. Momentum is the textbook case. Agents pile in simultaneously, and the return compresses before the second wave even arrives. Value signals, by contrast, require you to interpret accounting quality, and that interpretive step thins the competitive field. Fewer agents means slower compression and more residual capacity. Small-cap and illiquid anomalies retain alpha longer still, shielded by the limits to arbitrage: short-selling costs, capacity constraints, and the sheer friction of operating where large capital cannot easily go.

Green, Hand, and Zhang found that broad characteristics-based predictability essentially collapsed after 2003 outside of microcaps.<sup>[5]</sup> What survived was concentrated precisely where large capital could not easily go. If you want to know where the remaining alpha sits, start by looking at the places that charge the most to reach.

## The Crowding Spiral

Competition bleeds an edge slowly. You watch the alpha compress over years, paper by paper, replicator by replicator. Crowding kills in days.

Structurally similar positions, independently constructed, become correlated portfolios without anyone intending the overlap. During calm markets you cannot see that correlation. During a shock you cannot escape it, because one participant's forced liquidation pushes prices against every other fund holding the same factor tilt. Brunnermeier and Pedersen gave the loop its formal structure: falling asset prices raise margin requirements, margin calls force selling, selling pushes prices lower, and lower prices raise margin requirements again.<sup>[6]</sup> The densest crowding produces the fastest spiral.

If you study one crowding episode, make it August 2007. Amir Khandani and Andrew Lo built the forensic case from simulated long/short equity portfolios constructed on public valuation factors.<sup>[7]</sup> A forced-unwind sequence hit at 10:45 AM on August 1. Five days later, a second wave opened at the bell on August 6 and ran until 1:00 PM. One week. Market-neutral strategies that had compounded quietly for years posted extreme drawdowns inside it. Khandani and Lo called the mechanism the Unwind Hypothesis: a single large fund liquidated its long/short equity book to raise cash. Because dozens of quant shops held structurally similar positions, that one fund's forced selling landed as realized losses on every other book in the cluster.

Then the forced sellers ran out of inventory, and prices snapped back. Full reversion. A V-shaped recovery so clean it belonged in a textbook. That recovery is the part you should not skip, because it tells you exactly what happened and what did not. The underlying value signal was intact before the week and intact after it. No edge vanished. What destroyed capital was not a missing premium but an excess of participants holding the same premium at the same leverage, all of them discovering in the same five days that crowding converts a shared position into a shared loss.

Since 2007 the problem has gotten worse, not better. Meng and Chen measured it directly: 99.5 million holdings pulled from SEC 13F filings between 2013 and 2024, fed into a portfolio convergence model, and the output was a forty-two percent increase in simulated institutional overlap over the sample period.<sup>[8]</sup> Forty-two percent more crowding in roughly a decade. Shared data pipelines feed the convergence. Shared ML training sets accelerate it. Shared factor definitions lock it in. None of this means edges have disappeared. What it means is that the collective exposure to a single liquidation event, another August 2007 wearing a different date, keeps growing.

You can sometimes see it coming. Pojarliev and Levich, using CFTC position data, found that currency carry became measurably crowded six to twelve weeks before the October 2008 unwind, when USD/JPY moved roughly fifteen percent in days.<sup>[9]</sup> Kang, Rouwenhorst, and Tang showed that the returns to carry, value, and momentum strategies across commodity futures are highest when the trade is least crowded and lowest when it is most crowded.<sup>[10]</sup> Nearly all accumulated factor returns in their sample occurred during periods of low crowding. That is the arithmetic of a crowded trade: the mean return may still be positive, but the left tail grows fatter. A strategy that was once a smooth compounder becomes an episodic one, delivering returns in bursts separated by drawdowns that test whether your sizing can absorb the loss.

The lesson is not that crowding is avoidable. It is that crowding transforms the return distribution of a surviving edge. The edge does not vanish. It changes shape. And the shape it changes into is one that punishes anyone who sized for the distribution that used to hold.

## The Survival Table

Nine edge families, twelve chapters of evidence, and one question: what is still standing? The table below assembles every durability verdict in the book, mechanism by mechanism, with the evidence that produced it.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time-series momentum (Persistence) | 5 | Hardwired underreaction: news feeds into prices gradually, not instantly | Alive but episodic; decade-long droughts test conviction | Present in every decade tested; flat 2011 to 2019, sharp rescue 2022 |
| Cross-sectional momentum (the Shrinking Premium) | 7 | Replication compressed returns after publication | Compressed; substantially decayed post-publication | From roughly ten percent annually (1990s) to roughly two percent now |
| Mean reversion (Fading the Extreme) | 6 | Liquidity providers collect a reversal premium | Contested | Reversal present in high-volatility stocks; faster participants reach the premium first |

Start with the family that earned the longest look.

Every decade on record contains a period where trend-following worked. The SG CTA Index annualized at 4.8 percent from January 2000 through May 2024, a number that hides a brutal truth underneath it.<sup>[11]</sup> From 2011 to 2019, a full decade, the strategy went essentially nowhere. Then 2022 arrived, equities and bonds fell together, and the rescue was sharp enough to justify the prior eight years of drought for anyone who stayed. Behavioral underreaction to news is the mechanism, and it is hardwired rather than learned, which is why it keeps regenerating. Capacity is not the constraint: Baltas and Kosowski found no statistically significant capacity limits in CTA trend-following, consistent with the depth that futures markets offer relative to equity factor portfolios.<sup>[12]</sup> Crowding among trend programs is real. Quiet-regime Sharpe ratios are modest. But if you held through 2011 to 2019, you collected when the monetary regime broke. If you walked away during the drought, you missed the payout that made the family's survival visible.

Roughly ten percent annually in the 1990s, roughly two percent now. Cross-sectional momentum is the copying machine's cleanest exhibit.<sup>[13]</sup> McLean and Pontiff's fifty-eight percent post-publication decay maps onto this family almost exactly. A pocket of survival remains: factor momentum, applied across factor portfolios rather than individual stocks, holds up partly because fewer participants have figured out how to run it. Outside the United States, where arbitrage capital is thinner, international evidence shows stronger persistence. But notice the asymmetry before you build around two percent. The premium compressed. The drawdowns did not. You are weighing a shrunken return against unshrunken risk, and that ratio is the one the backtest tends to bury.

Contested is the verdict on mean reversion, and the reason is narrower than you might expect. Approximately 1.66 percent weekly in reversal premium still shows up in high-volatility, lottery-like stocks.<sup>[14]</sup> The reversal has not vanished. The mechanism underneath it, liquidity provision rewarded by a reversion in price, appears intact. What shifted is the queue. Faster participants reach the accessible share first. In liquid large-caps, the edge is consumed before your order arrives. You are not competing against the death of the signal. You are competing against the speed of the person who gets there one tick ahead of you.

No family in the book shows a starker decay arc than index reconstitution. From 7.4 percent in the 1990s to approximately 1.0 percent in the 2010s, statistically indistinguishable from zero: S&P 500 addition returns collapsed across two decades.<sup>[15]</sup> Greenwood and Sammon identified two structural forces behind the fall. First, a growing share of additions now migrate from the MidCap 400, and that migration generates matched buying and selling that cancels the demand shock. Second, arbitrage capital piled in until the front-runners were themselves being front-run, and eventually nobody remained to pay the premium. Russell reconstitution retains some structure: its rules-based announcement gives you time to pre-position. But crowding punished that exact crowd in 2022 with an eleven-percent reversal against them. MSCI emerging-market rebalancing is the pocket that still works, surviving precisely because thinner arbitrage infrastructure leaves the friction in place.

Alive and tail-risky. That is carry's permanent condition. Policy divergence creates interest-rate differentials, and the premium regenerates as long as the divergence holds. When differentials reverse, the premium collapses. You saw the cycle in miniature after 2022: the rate-hiking cycle opened large differentials between the United States and Japan, yen-funded carry positions swelled to an estimated forty trillion yen, and then a fifteen-basis-point Bank of Japan rate hike in August 2024 triggered the largest single-day VIX spike since 2020.<sup>[16]</sup> Positions were rebuilt by late 2024. The premium returned. That sequence, build, collapse, rebuild, is the signature of a structural edge whose crash risk is the binding constraint, not conviction. Ask yourself whether your sizing can absorb the unwind. If it cannot, the premium's existence is a fact about someone else's returns.

Nothing else in the table matches the volatility premium's persistence. Roughly eighty-five percent of months since 1990, implied volatility exceeded realized, across asset classes, not just equities.<sup>[17]</sup> The Sharpe ratios tell the same story from multiple angles: 0.6 in equities, 0.5 in fixed income and FX, 1.5 in commodities, 1.0 for a composite global strategy. The CBOE PUT index beat the S&P 500 on a Sharpe basis by forty-six percent over its full history. Why does this family resist decay when others crumble? Because the counterparty is not making an informational error. The counterparty is buying insurance against tail events, and overpaying for insurance is a preference, not a mistake. You cannot arbitrage a preference away. Protection, not return maximization, is the buyer's objective, and no published paper alters that objective. A buyer who already knows the cost and chooses to pay it anyway is not a buyer who will stop paying when the next study arrives.

Sixty-five to eighty-seven percent of mid-price variance at ten-to-sixty-second horizons, across NYSE and Nasdaq stocks. That is how much OFI explains, a result Cont, Kukanov, and Stoikov established and Kolm, Turiel, and Westray extended through 2023.<sup>[18]</sup> Approximately two average price changes. That is the effective alpha horizon. The signal itself shows no decay in the literature; it appears to be a structural feature baked into how electronic limit-order books function. What has narrowed is your access to it. The largest market makers internalized OFI into their own pricing, and if you are not co-located at the exchange, the accessible share of the signal shrinks with every hardware upgrade they install. For a systematic manager at slower speed, smart execution has become as much a defense against adverse selection as an alpha source in its own right.

Decay is not the right word for what happened to options-driven flows. Inversion is closer. Zero-day-to-expiry contracts grew from 0.08 million monthly in 2011 to 34.4 million by August 2023, now exceeding forty percent of all index option volume, and that explosion flipped the aggregate dealer book.<sup>[19]</sup> Where dealers once sat net long gamma, pinning prices toward dominant strikes through their hedging, many sessions now find them net short gamma, amplifying moves instead of dampening them. The old regime and the new one produce opposite price dynamics at the same venue. Charm-driven payoff bias at monthly expiry, however, has survived the flip: an estimated $2.5 to $3.5 billion in annual wealth transfer still passes through inflated S&P 500 opens on third-Friday expirations.<sup>[20]</sup> If you built a book around pinning, you are now sitting in the mirror image of the trade you designed for.

Two calendar effects, two different trajectories. The classic turn-of-month window has lost statistical significance in U.S. large-cap indices. International and small-cap markets still carry it, but the most visible version is gone. Quarter-end pension rebalancing is the one that matters, and it is a different kind of animal. Harvey, Mazzoleni, and Melone put a number on the cost: approximately sixteen billion dollars annually, with a seventeen-basis-point price impact on execution day.<sup>[21]</sup> Why does the flow persist when the research documenting it is public? Because the principal-agent problem behind it has never been addressed. The CIO knows the cost. The cost lands on beneficiaries, not on the CIO's compensation. No governance reform has separated those incentives, and until one does, the mandate stays exactly where it is. Publication is irrelevant to a structure that publication cannot alter.

## What the Table Reveals

Nine families, nine verdicts, and three patterns visible only when you read them together.

Start with the pattern that explains the most. Structural compulsion is the strongest predictor of survival. Corporate hedgers buy forward contracts whether or not the carry trade is crowded. Insurance buyers pay implied volatility above realized whether or not someone has published the premium. Passive funds trade at the effective date whether or not the price is favorable. Pension mandates compel quarter-end selling whether or not the CIO has read the research on its cost. Line up the survivors and you find the same architecture in each: a counterparty fulfilling a mandate. Not making an error. Fulfilling a mandate. Publication does not revise mandates.

Separate that from the behavioral edges, because the distinction matters. Behavioral persistence rests on the stability of human psychology: underreaction, overreaction, loss aversion. These are features of how individuals process information, and documenting them in a journal does not reprogram the processor. But a behavioral edge is at least theoretically crowdable. A sufficiently large, sufficiently disciplined counterforce that fully understands the underreaction mechanism and sizes correctly can, over enough time, compress the opportunity. Structural compulsion is not vulnerable to the same pressure. Understanding a mandate does not eliminate the mandate. A pension fund CIO who has read every paper on quarter-end rebalancing costs still rebalances at quarter-end. The mandate is not the CIO's to rewrite. Information, in this case, is irrelevant to the decision it is supposed to influence.

The second pattern runs in the opposite direction. Mechanical predictability and informational advantage are the fastest routes to decay. Cross-sectional momentum attracted simultaneous replication because the implementation is straightforward. Index reconstitution attracted front-running because the trade is fully legible from public data, and the capital chasing it grew faster than the underlying flow. The index addition premium went from significant to effectively zero inside two decades. Wherever a signal can be implemented by anyone with a data feed and a brokerage account, the competition is hyperbolic. Judgment-dependent signals, where the correct operationalization is itself contested, hold more capacity because fewer agents can compete effectively.

The distinction between mechanical and structural sharpens when you trace how the decay actually unfolds. A published mechanical signal can be implemented by the following morning by anyone with a data feed and a brokerage account. That is why the compression is not gradual. It arrives as a step function: the first wave of sophisticated replicators shows up, and the return drops before the second wave even reads the abstract. Think about the timeline in reverse. By the time a popular press article mentions the signal, the return may be statistically indistinguishable from zero. By the time the journal version appears, it has already compressed to a fraction. The pre-publication working paper readers harvested the early post-discovery window. The original researcher harvested most of the in-sample return. Each cohort entered at a lower expected payoff than the one before. What you call publication is not the cause of the compression. It is the event that makes the compression visible to the widest audience. The decay started the moment the first capable reader understood what the researcher had found.

The third pattern is the one you feel in the portfolio. Surviving edges do not hold still. They compress, shift regime, or migrate into niches that cost more to reach. Trend-following is alive and episodic, with decade-long droughts between payoffs. Carry is alive and tail-risky, with violent unwinds that arrive faster than any rebalancing schedule can absorb. Order flow is alive and gated by infrastructure, with the accessible share narrowing as co-location costs rise. Read the table as a landscape rather than a menu. The landscape moves.

Apply that to whatever you are running right now. If the edge family you trade is currently alive and accessible, it has not yet attracted enough capital to compress it to zero. By definition. That single observation should discipline every forecast you make about its future. The most dangerous conviction in trading is the belief that a strategy will persist because its track record is long. Track records do not cause persistence. Mechanisms do. An edge carries no institutional memory and does not know how many years it has been alive. Trend-following survived the 2011-to-2019 drought because the behavioral mechanism underneath it, institutional underreaction to sustained directional information, did not vanish when returns did. An unusual monetary regime suppressed the payoff. A changed monetary regime in 2022 released it. The survival was conditional on the mechanism's durability, not on the strategy's tenure.

## The Durable Edge

The opening chapter proposed a definition: a repeatable, positive-expectancy decision where the other side of the trade has a structural reason to accept the worse price. Twelve chapters of evidence later, the pattern that has emerged is not a menu of strategies you can copy. It is a principle about what separates the edges that last from the ones that do not.

Three conditions distinguish the edges that lasted from those that did not. First, the counterparty is compelled rather than merely wrong. Corporate hedgers buying forward contracts, pension funds selling at quarter-end, insurance buyers paying implied volatility above realized: each returns to the losing side of the trade next quarter and next year because a mandate requires it, not because a thesis persuades it. Second, the implementation resists mechanical replication. Complexity, speed, judgment, or physical constraints on the capital that can occupy the niche: at least one of these must stand between the signal and the crowd. Third, sizing must account for the regime that has not arrived yet. Every surviving edge carries a tail capable of consuming years of accumulated returns in days, and the tail does not announce itself in advance.

Map those three conditions onto the book's architecture and the hierarchy becomes visible. Part I covered knowledge and model edges, where survival depends almost entirely on the second condition: resisting replication. Published quantitative factors have failed here repeatedly and will continue to fail, because replication is trivially easy. Knowledge edges persist only in their most judgment-dependent forms, where translating information into a position requires domain competence no algorithm can substitute for. Part II covered behavioral edges, where survival rests on the stability of the first condition's mechanism: the psychological bias that creates the losing counterparty. Behavioral edges outlast model edges because information does not correct the bias. An investor who understands the disposition effect still experiences the pain of a realized loss more acutely than the regret of a missed gain. Knowing the wiring does not change the wiring. But behavioral edges are not as durable as structural ones. They are at least theoretically crowdable, and the institutional infrastructure that was supposed to eliminate them, quantitative funds, systematic trend-followers, volatility arbitrageurs, has in practice compressed them over three decades. Part III covered structural edges, where the first condition is hardest to satisfy and hardest to erode: the counterparty is compelled, not biased. The mandate is the mechanism. The mandate does not decay.

Every edge follows a lifecycle: discovery, exploitation, crowding, decay. Most complete the arc. The survivors are the families where the losing counterparty is replenished by forces that sit outside the market's information structure entirely. Mandates. Regulations. Risk preferences. Behavioral tendencies that no publication can correct. Trend-following survives because underreaction to news is behavioral rather than informational. Carry survives because hedging demand is contractual. The volatility premium survives because the demand for insurance is a preference rather than a mistake.

That hierarchy, mechanical at the bottom, behavioral in the middle, structural at the top, organizes the book. It also works as a diagnostic you can carry forward. When someone presents you with a claimed edge, skip the backtest. Ask what replenishes the losing side. If the answer is nothing specific, the pattern just works, the edge is mechanical and will decay. If the answer is human psychology, which is stable, the edge has behavioral roots and will compress but probably persist. If the answer is a mandate, a regulation, an institutional objective function that no individual participant can revise, the edge is structural and will endure as long as the institution that generates it endures.

No guarantee in the public record promises that any particular edge will survive the next decade. McLean and Pontiff's post-publication decay is an average, and averages conceal the spread: some edges decay faster, some slower, and some transform into new risk rather than vanishing. What the evidence does establish is a direction. Look toward the structural. Look away from the mechanical. And always ask who is on the other side of the trade and whether they have the ability to stop.

What survives longest is not a strategy. It is a discipline. Size small when conviction is uncertain. Adapt when the regime shifts. Release the edges that have died rather than averaging into them. Risk management, the subject of the prior chapter, is not a supplement to edge. It is the infrastructure that keeps edge alive through decay, crowding, and regime change. Strip that infrastructure away and even the most robust premium becomes a route to ruin. August 2007 did not destroy the underlying value signal. The signal was intact before the crisis and intact after it. What August 2007 destroyed was every trader who held that signal at leverage the drawdown could not forgive.

## Key Points

- Publication compresses forward alpha by more than half on average; mechanical signals decay fastest because they attract the most replication.
- Crowding transforms surviving edges by fattening the left tail rather than eliminating the mean return, as the August 2007 quant meltdown demonstrated.
- Structural edges, where the counterparty is compelled by mandate rather than misinformed, have proved the most resistant to decay across all nine families.
- The Volatility Premium and carry premium persist because the demand for insurance and hedging regenerates independently of market awareness.
- Survival requires sizing for the regime that has not yet arrived, because every durable edge carries a tail capable of consuming years of accumulated returns.

The edge is real. It survives. But it does not wait.
