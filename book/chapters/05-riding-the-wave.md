# Chapter 5: Riding the Wave

*The SG CTA Index returned 4.8 percent annualized from January 2000 to May 2024. That number contains an eight-year flat stretch, a spectacular 2022, and enough whipsaw to break most allocators. Trend-following survives anyway. The behavioral root is underreaction to news, a structural delay in how large pools of capital absorb information, and no amount of crowding has fully arbitraged it away. Persistence names both the phenomenon these systems capture and the quality you need to collect it.*

## Six Speeds

Robert Carver's EWMAC framework does not pick a trend speed. It runs six at once. Six exponentially weighted moving average crossover pairs, 2:8, 4:16, 8:32, 16:64, 32:128, and 64:256, each maintaining a four-to-one ratio between its fast and slow lookback windows.<sup>[1]</sup> The raw crossover signal, fast EWMA minus slow EWMA, is divided by the recent standard deviation of price changes so that forecasts are comparable across instruments with wildly different volatilities. Then each pair gets its own forecast scalar: 10.6 for the 2:8 pair, 7.5 for 4:16, 5.3 for 8:32, 3.75 for 16:64, 2.65 for 32:128, and 1.87 for the slowest, 64:256.<sup>[1]</sup> A multiplier that normalizes signal strength so a forecast of ten from the fastest pair carries the same positional weight as a forecast of ten from the slowest.

Cap at twenty. Position size: forecast multiplied by a volatility scalar, divided by ten.<sup>[1]</sup>

Watch what you are actually holding. The fastest pair, 2:8, reacts to price changes over the last two to eight trading days, catches a sharp move within a week, and whipsaws through every minor pullback just as fast. The slowest pair, 64:256, ignores anything shorter than three months. It rides a year-long trend without flinching but will not register a sharp two-week rally at all. Six speeds running simultaneously, each one a bet that trends exist at the frequency it measures, none of them a commitment that any single frequency is the right one to trust with your account.

The forecast scalars do the work you would never see. Without them, the fastest pair dominates the combined forecast through sheer noise rather than signal quality, and the position lurches on every two-day reversal that the slower pairs would have filtered out. The scalars compress that range so each speed contributes roughly equally. The cap at twenty prevents an outsized bet, even when all six agree.

Why six rather than three, or twelve? The four-to-one ratio between fast and slow windows means adjacent pairs overlap in the frequency band they capture, but not so much that they are redundant. The 2:8 catches the two-week rally that the 8:32 is too slow for. The 64:256 rides the year-long grind that the 32:128 has already exited. Six spans days to months without the system becoming so complex that the interactions between pairs turn unpredictable.

**Reconstruction.** Six concurrent bets on trend speed sound like diversification. They are also six concurrent bets that trends exist at all. When markets chop, each pair generates its own sequence of whipsaws at its own frequency, and the combined forecast oscillates around zero while the account absorbs the cumulative cost of six sets of losing trades. The diversification is across speeds, not across outcomes. All six share one assumption: that prices trend. When they do not, all six lose.

When all six agree, the system concentrates. The cap limits the forecast, but the position is still directional, and a reversal hits all six at once. The fast pair exits first. The slow pair keeps holding. The drawdown between the first exit and the last is the price of carrying six speeds, paid precisely when the regime shifts. The alternative, one speed, is cheaper in a reversal and catastrophic if you chose the wrong frequency. That trade-off has no resolution. It has a design choice.

What would disprove the multi-speed thesis is a market where trend persistence clustered tightly around a single frequency. If momentum existed only at the three-month horizon, five of the six pairs would be dead weight. No such clustering has appeared in the published data.

**What it takes.** The whole architecture rests on a futures account that can hold six concurrent positions per instrument across a diversified book. Six forecast scalars, from 10.6 down to 1.87, each calibrated to its own lookback pair and recalibrated when the volatility denominator drifts. A volatility estimate updated at least daily, because the position-sizing formula divides by it. The cap at twenty limits the combined bet, but when all six pairs agree in a reversal, the drawdown between the fastest pair's exit and the slowest pair's exit is the cost you cannot hedge. A single-instrument version of this is a demonstration, not a strategy.

## One Moving Average Cross

Andreas Clenow took the entire concept and stripped it to one frequency. His Starter System runs one crossover: the 16-day moving average crossing above the 64-day triggers a long entry; crossing below triggers a short.<sup>[2]</sup> One constraint: no repeat trades in the same direction until the moving average reverses. He selected the 16/64 pair after backtesting multiple combinations, balancing trade frequency against costs, verified it on five instruments, then live-tested it from June through October 2018.<sup>[2]</sup>

Two parameters. That is it. A two-parameter system leaves almost nothing to optimize, which means almost nothing to overfit. Hand an optimizer enough parameters and enough historical data and it will find patterns that existed only in the sample, a system that looks brilliant in backtests and fails live. Two parameters cannot hide that kind of noise-fitting. The system either works because momentum is real, or it does not work at all.

The no-repeat-trade rule does more than you might expect. Without it, price hovering near the crossover point generates a long signal, an immediate reversal to short, an immediate reversal back to long, each time incurring transaction costs, each time wrong. The rule forces you to wait for a genuine reversal of the moving average relationship before re-entering. You sit out the noise. The cost is occasionally missing a new trend that begins with a brief whipsaw. The benefit is avoiding a cascade of small losses that erodes months of performance in a single week of chop.

**Reconstruction.** Carver's six-speed architecture, reduced to one frequency, is the Starter System. The scalars, the cap, the diversification across lookback windows: gone. What remains is a single moving average pair in the middle of Carver's range, roughly the 16:64 EWMAC speed, and a rule that says follow the cross.

The 16/64 sits at a sweet spot. Faster systems are harder to distinguish from noise. Slower ones offer fewer trades per year. Medium-frequency persistence, one to three months, appears to be the dominant wavelength of directional price movement across many markets. Carver and Clenow arrived at it from different directions.

The live test covered four months. Far too short to confirm edge. Long enough to confirm execution feasibility: signals fire cleanly, positions can be sized without slippage distorting results, the no-repeat rule works in practice. The deeper question is whether the gains from simplicity, lower costs, lower cognitive load, lower overfitting risk, outweigh what you lose by ignoring the faster and slower frequencies Carver captures. For a retail trader with limited capital and a single-instrument focus, the trade-off may favor simplicity. For a fund trading dozens of instruments across multiple asset classes, multi-speed captures the crisis alpha a single frequency cannot reach. The stripping away clarifies the bet. It does not improve it.

**What it takes.** No multi-asset infrastructure is involved, which is precisely where the system stops scaling. A brokerage account that supports short selling, a daily price feed for five or fewer instruments, and a spreadsheet that computes two moving averages. The four-month live test confirmed execution feasibility, not edge, so the first year of live signals is a cost with no guaranteed return. When price hovers near the crossover point, the no-repeat rule forces you to sit out the chop.

## The Behavioral Tax

Clenow's 2009 tells you more about trend-following than any backtest can.<sup>[3]</sup> That year was trendless, choppy, the kind of market that moves sideways with enough noise to trigger entry signals and enough reversals to hit stops before any trend can develop. His signals produced stop-out after stop-out on the same instruments. Same market. Same entry rule. Same stop. Same loss. Again. And again.

Every consecutive loss increased the psychological pressure to skip the next trade, to override the system just this once, to wait for confirmation that the regime had shifted before committing capital to another signal that felt identical to the six that had just failed.

He kept taking every signal.<sup>[3]</sup>

The reasoning is arithmetic, not willpower. A trend-following system derives most of its annual return from a small number of large winning trades. The win rate runs below fifty percent, sometimes well below. Skip the seventh trade because the previous six were losers, and if the seventh is the one that trends, you have paid the full cost of the flat period without collecting any of its payoff. The edge comes not from being right most of the time but from making far more on the winners than you lose on the losers. Missing even one of those rare large winners can turn a profitable year flat, or a flat year into a losing one.

After six consecutive losses, your estimate of the next trade's probability drops. The losses are concrete and recent. The future winning trade is abstract and uncertain. But that revision is a bias, not a calculation. The next trade's probability of success is determined by the market's regime, not by the outcome of the previous six trades. If the system's edge exists and the market has shifted from choppy to trending, the next signal carries the same expected value as any other signal the system has ever produced. The prior losses tell you about the recent past. They say nothing about the next trade.

**Reconstruction.** The trend-following parameters are public. The EWMAC lookbacks, the crossover pairs, the volatility scalars: published, replicable, available to anyone with a spreadsheet and a library card. Yet the edge persists. That persistence demands an explanation, and the explanation is not in the formula.

It is in the flat years. The eight-year stretch from 2011 to 2019 was this dynamic at industry scale. Eight years of whipsaw that drove assets out of trend-following funds, compressed allocations, forced closures, pushed the strategy to the margins of institutional portfolio construction. The undisciplined left. Markets got less crowded. When 2022 delivered the commodity and rate trends that rewarded persistence, the survivors found fewer competitors.

That is not a motivational story. It is a selection mechanism. The behavioral tax, the willingness to absorb repeated losses and take the next signal, functions as a barrier to entry that no published research can remove. You can read the formula. You can code the system. You can run the backtest. What you cannot do is sit through 2009 and then watch the flat stretch run from 2011 to 2019 and still take the next signal when it fires. The falsifier is plain: if capital ever stopped cycling out during flat periods, the barrier would dissolve and the returns with it. That has not happened.

**What it takes.** You are sizing each trade to survive six or more consecutive stop-outs without breaching the account's risk limit. The win rate runs below fifty percent, sometimes well below, and most of the annual return comes from a small number of large winners. Skip one of those winners and the year turns flat or negative. A 2009-style trendless stretch produces months of repeated small losses on the same instruments with the same rules, and every loss increases the pressure to deviate from the next signal. The cost is not in the account. It is in taking the seventh trade after the sixth one failed.

## When the System Breaks

Late 2008 put a number on where systematic discipline cracks. Clenow described accumulated profits reaching 50 to 70 percent of the fund's value while daily fund swings hit 10 to 20 percent.<sup>[4]</sup> The trend-following system was working, spectacularly, with the models positioned correctly for the crash: short equities, long bonds, and the profits were enormous. But the volatility itself had moved outside the parameters the system was designed for. Daily swings of 15 to 20 percent meant that a single adverse day could erase weeks of gains in a portfolio that had been built to capture exactly the kind of directional persistence the crash was delivering. The volatility-scaling mechanism, which sizes positions based on recent volatility, was lagging behind the actual regime shift because the inputs it used to estimate volatility had never seen anything like this.

His decision: reduce all positions by discretionary override.<sup>[4]</sup>

Read that against the previous section. The entire premise of trend-following is that you follow the system, that human intervention introduces the very biases the system was built to eliminate, and that skipping a signal or overriding a position is the one thing you are not supposed to do. The discipline case says take every signal. This case says override the system entirely. Both come from the same practitioner.

Clenow did not resolve the contradiction. He named it. The strategy's design parameters had a boundary, and late 2008 sat outside it.<sup>[4]</sup> Protecting 50 to 70 percent in accumulated profit mattered more than staying faithful to the next signal. That is not a systematic rule. It is a judgment that the regime had broken, that no historical data the system trained on contained anything resembling this, and that preserving the capital trumped preserving the process.

**Reconstruction.** The crisis override is discipline relocated from the signal to the account. One protects the process. The other protects the capital the process exists to compound. No formula marks the boundary between them. A system is designed for a regime. When the regime changes fast enough and far enough, the system's assumptions may no longer hold. But the system cannot recognize this, because the change is happening outside its parameter space. The volatility model says current volatility is high. It cannot say its own estimates are unreliable. That judgment requires a meta-awareness the model does not possess.

The thresholds Clenow described, 50 to 70 percent accumulated profit with 10 to 20 percent daily swings, are not backtest outputs. They are judgment calls made in real time by a practitioner who recognized that the rules had temporarily changed. Most practitioners will never see daily swings of that magnitude. Those who do will face the same choice: trust the system or protect the capital. The temptation is to see abnormality everywhere, which dissolves back into discretionary trading by another name. The threshold must fire only in genuine regime breaks, not in ordinary drawdowns that feel extreme while they are happening. What would disprove the need for override is a volatility model that adjusts fast enough to size positions correctly through a break of this magnitude. No such model exists in the public record.

**What it takes.** If daily swings reach 10 to 20 percent of fund value, the volatility-scaling model is estimating from data that no longer describes the current regime. The override Clenow described was a discretionary reduction of all positions while accumulated profits sat at 50 to 70 percent. That decision has no backtest. It cannot be coded as a rule without creating a new system that itself has untested boundary conditions. Running a systematic strategy through a regime break requires a pre-committed threshold for intervention, defined before the crisis, and the willingness to accept that the threshold will look either premature or late and never correct.

## The Bond Offset

Forty percent from bonds alone. That was the number in 2008, and it came from a system that had no opinion about bonds whatsoever.<sup>[5]</sup> While equity markets collapsed, Clenow's trend-following system held long positions in government bonds and interest rate futures, not because it had predicted the crisis or analyzed the subprime mortgage market or modeled the Lehman balance sheet, but because it had followed the trend in bonds, which was up, while the trend in equities was down. Capital fled risk assets for safe havens. Central banks slashed rates. Those bond positions rallied hard.<sup>[5]</sup>

The system was also short equities during the crash, and those positions were profitable too. But the bond leg alone generated 40 percent. Not a hedge. Not a defensive allocation designed to cushion the portfolio against a scenario somebody anticipated. An active, trend-following position in bonds that happened to trend sharply because the crisis drove capital into government debt at a pace nobody had modeled and the formula simply followed. The system treated bonds exactly the way it treated equities, currencies, and commodities: follow the price. The crisis supplied the direction.

You can see the mechanism without a macroeconomic model of any kind. Capital rotates out of risk assets and into government debt during a crisis, bond prices rise as yields fall, and a trend-following system holding positions across multiple asset classes ends up long bonds during an equity crash because bonds are trending upward while equities are trending downward. No understanding of why the trend exists is required. No view on monetary policy. Price contains the information, and the system reads price.

That structural difference separates multi-asset trend-following from equity-only momentum entirely. An equity-only system goes long stocks or sits in cash. During a crash, you have nothing generating positive returns to offset equity losses, nothing trending in your favor while the rest of the portfolio bleeds. A multi-asset system holds positions in bonds, currencies, commodities, and interest rate futures, and in a crisis several of these asset classes tend to trend sharply. The system is already positioned in them.

**Reconstruction.** "Crisis alpha" is a polite label for something simpler: being long whatever everyone is panic-buying and short whatever everyone is panic-selling, at scale, because a formula told you to be there before the panic started. The formula did not anticipate the crisis. It followed a price trend that existed for entirely mundane reasons, rate cuts and safe-haven flows, and the crisis merely accelerated what the formula was already riding. The alpha is not in the prediction. It is in the positioning, and the positioning is a mechanical consequence of running trend signals across enough asset classes that at least one of them trends violently when equities break.

Clenow measured the correlation between trend-following and equities at minus 0.16 over the 1990 to 2011 backtest period.<sup>[6]</sup> Small number. But small and negative is the most valuable correlation in portfolio mathematics, because the negative return arrives precisely when you need the offset most. A bond position generating 40 percent during the worst equity year in a generation is worth far more to your portfolio than the same 40 percent during a calm year when equities are also compounding. The crisis makes the correlation worth having. The system makes it accessible. What would prove this wrong is a crisis where bonds and equities fell together, sustained and correlated. That has not yet appeared in the data this system was tested on.

**What it takes.** Forty percent from bonds in a single year, and the system that captured it was not a bond strategy. It was a multi-asset futures account holding positions in government bonds, interest rate futures, currencies, and commodities alongside equities. An equity-only momentum system cannot produce this offset because it has no bond positions to rally during the crash. The correlation measured at minus 0.16 over 1990 to 2011, which means the offset is structural but not guaranteed in magnitude. Accessing it requires a futures account, margin for positions across multiple asset classes, and the willingness to hold bond longs that produce nothing during calm years when equities are rising steadily.

## The Allocation Question

The efficient frontier gave a number, and the number was 30 to 40 percent. Clenow ran the analysis across 1990 to 2011 and found that allocating 30 to 40 percent of a portfolio to diversified trend-following futures, with the remaining 60 to 70 percent in MSCI World equities, produced higher returns and lower volatility than either component alone.<sup>[6]</sup> Monthly rebalancing maintained the target allocation.<sup>[6]</sup>

Minus 0.16. That was the correlation between the futures trend-following strategy and the equity benchmark. A correlation that low means the two return streams move largely independently, and occasionally in opposite directions. You can feel why that matters without portfolio mathematics: when one component falls, the other is not falling with it, and sometimes it is rising. Combining two assets with low or negative correlation reduces the portfolio's overall volatility more than either asset's own risk would predict, because the losses in one tend to be partially offset by stability or gains in the other.

Below 30 percent, the equity component dominates the portfolio's behavior and the trend-following allocation is too small to meaningfully offset equity drawdowns. You are paying for a diversifier that cannot diversify. Above 40 percent, the portfolio begins to take on the return profile of the trend-following component, including its eight-year flat periods, which most investors cannot tolerate. The 30 to 40 percent band sits on the efficient frontier, the curve that maps the best attainable return for each level of risk, at the point where diversification benefit and tracking-error cost are roughly in balance.

**Reconstruction.** The allocation traced forward through time stops looking like a portfolio construction exercise and starts looking like a behavioral filter operating at portfolio scale. A 35 percent allocation to trend-following during 2011 to 2019 would have dragged combined returns below a pure equity portfolio for nearly a decade. Every quarter, the equity-only benchmark outperformed the blended portfolio, and every quarter the pressure to cut the trend-following allocation grew. The investor who held through that decade collected the crisis alpha in 2020 and the trend returns in 2022. The investor who abandoned the allocation in 2017 collected neither.

That is the behavioral tax relocated from the trading account to the portfolio. At the trade level, the willingness to take the seventh signal after six consecutive losses. At the portfolio level, the willingness to rebalance monthly into the component that has been lagging for years. Selling what just worked. Buying what just failed. The arithmetic says this is how you capture the correlation benefit. Your recent performance says this is how you fall further behind. Same mechanism. Same psychology. If investors ever stopped abandoning trend-following during flat stretches, the crowding in calm years would compress the returns available to those who stayed, and the behavioral premium would shrink toward zero. That has not happened either.

**What it takes.** A futures account large enough to hold 30 to 40 percent of total capital in diversified trend-following positions across bonds, currencies, commodities, and equity indices, with the remaining 60 to 70 percent in MSCI World equities. Monthly rebalancing to maintain the target split, which means selling the component that outperformed and buying the one that lagged, the opposite of what feels right after a divergent quarter. The minus 0.16 correlation was measured over 1990 to 2011 and may not hold at the same magnitude in future regimes, so the allocation rests on a structural relationship whose size is uncertain. Margin requirements for the futures leg scale with the number of instruments traded.

## Equity Momentum, Distilled

The whole concept fits in a single rule: go long when a stock closes above its 100-day moving average, exit when it falls below.<sup>[7]</sup> The portfolio holds thirty positions at equal weight, roughly 3.3 percent each, rebalanced monthly across the S&P 500 universe.<sup>[7]</sup>

Two sentences to describe it. An afternoon to implement it. The 100-day moving average separates upward-trending stocks from downward-trending ones. Equal weighting prevents any single position from dominating the portfolio. Monthly rebalancing limits turnover so that transaction costs stay manageable. You could hand this system to someone with no market experience and they could run it from a spreadsheet. No questions about what the market is doing or why.

The 100-day window is roughly five months of trading history. Long enough to filter out daily noise. Short enough to respond to genuine regime changes within a quarter or two. A stock sitting above its 100-day average is, by definition, in an uptrend measured at the quarterly-to-semiannual frequency. The momentum effect confirmed by decades of academic research and practitioner experience is the assumption underneath: stocks above this threshold have a higher probability of continuing to rise than stocks below it.

Look at what the equal-weight construction does at each monthly rebalance. Stocks that have risen disproportionately get trimmed back to their 3.3 percent target, while stocks that have merely held above the moving average receive proportionally more capital. A mild contrarian force inside a momentum framework. The biggest recent winners are systematically trimmed, which reduces your exposure to the most extended names without abandoning the momentum thesis entirely.

**Reconstruction.** The 100-day equity filter is the EWMAC framework with five of its six speeds removed and its short leg amputated. Both wager that prices trend. Both use a moving average to define the trend. The 100-day window captures roughly the same frequency as the 16:64 or 32:128 EWMAC pair. The difference is in what surrounds the core bet, not in the bet itself.

What surrounds it is everything the equity-only version strips away. No short positions, so you cannot profit from declining markets. No bond leg, so you cannot capture the 40 percent crisis offset Clenow's multi-asset system delivered in 2008. No currency or commodity exposure, so the diversification that dampens whipsaw across the multi-asset book is absent entirely. For a long-only equity investor who accepts those constraints, the system provides a disciplined framework for staying in rising stocks and exiting falling ones, which is most of what trend-following delivers in equities. It will prevent you from holding stocks falling through their moving averages, and that avoidance is where most of the drawdown protection lives. What would show the simplification carries no cost is a long equity-only backtest that matches the risk-adjusted return of a multi-asset trend system. No such comparison exists in the source.

**What it takes.** Budget for thirty concurrent equity positions at roughly 3.3 percent each, rebalanced monthly across the S&P 500 universe. The system is long-only, so there is no shorting requirement and no futures margin, but there is also no mechanism to profit from declining markets. During a crash, the system steps aside rather than generating the bond offset or short-equity returns that a multi-asset futures approach captures. A brokerage account, a daily price feed covering the S&P 500, and a monthly rebalance routine that trims winners back to equal weight. The gap between this and a multi-asset futures system is the crisis alpha you are choosing not to collect.

## The Regime Gate

One trading author proposed the 200-week moving average as the only directional filter you need across crypto, forex, and stock markets.<sup>[8]</sup> Roughly four years of price history, compressed into a single line on a chart, and every trade decision starts by asking which side of it you are on. Binary rule. Price above the 200-week average means you look for longs only. Price below means shorts only. Counter-trend trades are explicitly excluded.<sup>[8]</sup>

The logic runs deeper than a chart pattern. A trend measured over roughly four years reflects not market sentiment but fundamental economic forces: interest rate regimes, secular growth trends, structural shifts in capital flows that rearrange entire asset classes while shorter signals flicker and reverse. Trading against a trend of that duration is, according to this approach, a negative-expectancy proposition over time. Accept that constraint and you eliminate an entire category of trades that carry unfavorable odds. The cost is real: when price sits near the average and direction is ambiguous, you have nothing to trade. But the trades you skip are the ones that would have placed your capital against a four-year structural current.

Notice what the filter does not do. It does not tell you when to enter. It does not tell you when to exit. It tells you which direction to face. A stock above its 200-week average qualifies for long entries only, and you then apply whatever shorter-term signal, a crossover, a breakout, a pullback to support, to time the actual trade. Below the 200-week average, shorts only. Pre-condition. Not a strategy.

**Reconstruction.** What would break this filter is a regime change that unfolds faster than four years of price data can register. A central bank reversing the direction of rates inside a single cycle rewrites the structural trend while the 200-week average still reflects the old regime. The filter keeps you positioned in the direction that was correct two years ago, not the direction that is correct now. The faster the reversal, the longer the filter lags, and the lag is not a bug in the implementation. It is the cost of using four years of history as your compass.

The evidence quality compounds the problem. No backtest supports the 200-week window. The case for it is asserted, resting on the plausible but unverified intuition that longer trends outlast shorter ones. Momentum research lends broad support, and the roughly four-year horizon tends to coincide with business-cycle turning points in equity indices. Neither of those observations validates the specific parameter. Carver's EWMAC pairs and Clenow's 16/64 cross have been tested against live data and published with results attached. The 200-week filter has not. Treat it as a constraint that removes a large class of likely unprofitable trades, not as an edge in its own right. Whatever edge survives lives downstream, in the timing signal that determines when to act within the direction the filter permits.

**What it takes.** What ends most attempts here is not the filter itself but the dead zone around it. Price can sit near the 200-week average for months, generating no qualifying entries in either direction. A weekly price feed going back four years, a single moving average calculation, and a shorter-term signal to time entries within the permitted direction. The 200-week parameter is asserted rather than backtested, so the filter is a constraint on which trades to consider, not a tested source of edge.

## Reading the Coil

Pull up Tesla in 2025 and you can watch the whole thesis happen on one screen. A trading desk analyst flagged converging moving averages, six different lookback windows all tightening toward the same price, compressing into what traders call a coil.<sup>[9]</sup> Short-term averages climbing. Long-term averages going flat. The gap between them shrinking to almost nothing, day after day, until the chart looked like a rubber band twisted to its limit. Sitting just above that coil: the 200-day moving average, the line the entire industry uses to separate bullish territory from bearish. The analyst's read was specific: a breakout above the 200-day average would confirm the larger uptrend, initial targets in the 340 to 350 range.<sup>[9]</sup>

On Friday, the breakout arrived.

You have seen this before, in different notation. Carver quantifies convergence through exponential decay parameters. Clenow codes it as an explicit MA cross. The analyst on this desk looked at the same chart and read the same convergence visually: moving averages compressing toward each other signal shrinking directional disagreement, and a breakout above a major average resolves that disagreement upward. Three formats for one piece of information. The systematic version outputs a number. The discretionary version produces a shape on a screen. Both place the same directional bet.

**Reconstruction.** The coil pattern, extended to its logical conclusion, is a convergence trade with no statistical foundation underneath it. The targets, 340 to 350, are specific numbers that give you the appearance of precision, but they rest on chart reading rather than a model with defined confidence intervals. The discretionary trader synthesizes visual pattern recognition, sector context, and timing judgment into a single decision, and that synthesis is the claim. If the judgment is replicable across thousands of trades, the discretionary version carries something a formula cannot capture: the ability to read context, to see the coil forming before the crossover confirms it, and to size the position based on the quality of the setup rather than a fixed rule.

Whether that judgment is replicable, or whether it is an artifact of hindsight bias applied to the trades that happened to work, is the question that separates a repeatable skill from a highlight reel. The systematic trader's advantage is consistency: the same rule, applied the same way, on every signal, regardless of whether the last six signals were winners or losers. The discretionary trader's potential advantage is flexibility. Extend the flexibility claim far enough and it becomes unfalsifiable, because every losing trade can be reframed as a setup read incorrectly rather than evidence the method does not work. The only resolution is a track record long enough to distinguish skill from selection. No such record appears in the source.

**What it takes.** You are trading a single name on a single chart with price targets derived from visual pattern recognition, not from a statistical model. The 340 to 350 target range rests on chart reading without defined confidence intervals, so the position size cannot be justified by a backtest distribution. Replicating this across thousands of trades is the open question: whether the timing judgment that identified the Tesla coil is a repeatable skill or a hindsight selection from the setups that happened to work. No systematic infrastructure is required. What is required is a track record long enough to distinguish skill from selection bias, and that record does not exist in the source.

## The Industry's Dilemma

Fifty or more futures instruments, moving average crossovers, breakout rules, leverage, and the discipline to take every signal regardless of recent performance. That is the Commodity Trading Advisor business, the professional class of systematic trend-followers, and it runs the institutional version of every concept in this chapter.<sup>[10]</sup> Multi-speed signals. Multi-asset diversification. Systematic execution. The behavioral tax, paid at scale.

Start with the number. The SG CTA Index returned 4.8 percent annualized from January 2000 through May 2024.<sup>[10]</sup> Twenty-four years of live trading, real capital at risk, through the 2008 financial crisis, the 2020 pandemic, and everything that chopped sideways in between. That 4.8 percent includes 2022, the year the post-pandemic inflation surge sent commodities and interest rates on the kind of sustained directional move these systems were built for. You could feel the vindication from across the industry. Trend-following exists for exactly that scenario, major economic forces pushing prices in one direction long enough for a mechanical signal to ride.

Now turn it over. The decade from 2011 to 2019 sits inside that 4.8 percent, and during those years CTA returns went nowhere. Markets chopped sideways. Central bank intervention kept volatility compressed. Meanwhile the pool of CTA capital kept growing, which meant a hundred funds were running the same signals on the same liquid futures contracts, entering at roughly the same time and exiting at roughly the same time. The collective buying pressure on entry pushed the fill price higher. The collective selling pressure on exit pushed it lower. Each trend that would have been profitable for a single fund was less profitable when the crowd piled in alongside you.

The crowding is real. It has not destroyed the edge, but it has compressed it. You can see the mechanism, and you cannot dodge it.

**Reconstruction.** If trend-following captures underreaction, and underreaction is durable, who exactly is paying for the return? That is the question the 4.8 percent annualized figure quietly avoids answering. New information arrives. Prices adjust, but partially. The remaining adjustment creates the trend. This underreaction is not a quirk of retail traders or a temporary inefficiency that better technology will eliminate. It is a feature of institutional decision-making: committees move slowly, mandates constrain timing, risk budgets limit position sizes, and the collective machinery of professional asset management introduces structural delays between the arrival of information and its full expression in price. Those frictions are embedded in the architecture of how large pools of capital are managed.

But the capital chasing that underreaction has grown. The more funds that follow trends, the faster the initial adjustment occurs, which reduces the trend's magnitude. Crowding compresses the payoff from each trend because a hundred funds entering the same position simultaneously accelerate the entry price and worsen the exit. The crowding also creates a secondary source of trend: the eventual unwinding of crowded positions itself produces a move in the opposite direction, captured by the next cycle of signals. The decade from 2011 to 2019 was not an accident. It was the market adjusting to the weight of capital deployed in the strategy. No amount of CTA capital has fully arbitraged the mechanism away, because the arbitrage itself requires taking the same positions that create the trends.

**What it takes.** Fifty or more futures instruments across asset classes, each carrying its own margin requirement and its own liquidity profile. Moving average crossovers and breakout rules generate the signals, leverage amplifies the exposure, and the same entry logic every other CTA runs on the same liquid contracts means your fills worsen in proportion to the crowd around you. The 4.8 percent annualized over twenty-four years is a live number, not a backtest, earned across multiple regime changes. Running this program requires capital that can absorb years of flat returns without triggering redemptions, execution infrastructure that scales across dozens of instruments simultaneously, and an investment mandate that will not be revoked after the third consecutive year of lagging a passive equity benchmark.

## Key Points

- Trend-following captures residual price adjustment after markets partially absorb new information; this behavioral root has not been arbitraged away.
- Running multiple signal speeds converts timing uncertainty into frequency diversification, catching fast and slow trends without committing to either.
- The willingness to take the next signal after consecutive losses is itself an edge, because flat years shake out competitors who refuse to pay that cost.
- Crisis alpha is a natural consequence of multi-asset trend-following, not a bolt-on feature; the system ends up short equities and long bonds during panics.
- CTA crowding has compressed returns, but live systematic trend programs still show positive performance over two decades of real trading.

Trend-following profits from underreaction: the market's failure to price in change quickly enough. But what happens when the pendulum swings too far? The next chapter explores mean reversion, fading the extreme, and the behavioral overreaction that makes it work.
